**1. Problem reformulation**

For a step function \(h:[0,2]\to[0,1]\) with \(\int_0^2 h(x)\,dx=1\) define the discrete sampling
\[
h_i\approx h\!\left(\tfrac{2i}{n}\right),\qquad i=0,\dots,n-1,
\]
with step size \(\Delta =2/n\).  
The discrete analogue of the Erdős overlap is
\[
C_5(h)=\Delta\; \max_{k\in\{-n+1,\dots,n-1\}} 
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+k}\bigr)\,, 
\]
where out‑of‑range indices are ignored.  

Because \(\sum_i h_i\Delta=1\) is constant,
\[
C_5(h)=\Delta\Bigl(\sum_i h_i -\min_{k}\sum_i h_i\,h_{i+k}\Bigr)
   = \Delta\Bigl(\tfrac{n}{2}-\min_{k}\rho_k\Bigr)
\]
with the autocorrelation \(\rho_k=\sum_i h_i\,h_{i+k}\).  
Thus **minimising \(C_5\) is equivalent to maximising the minimal autocorrelation** \(\min_k\rho_k\).

The objective is a non‑smooth max over the vector
\[
c_k = \Delta\sum_i h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+k}\bigr) .
\]
All constraints are simple box constraints together with the affine sum constraint
\[
0\le h_i\le 1,\qquad \sum_i h_i = \tfrac{n}{2}.
\]

**2. Smoothed max (soft‑max) surrogate**

Replace the hard max by a temperature‑controlled soft‑max:
\[
F_\varepsilon(h)=\varepsilon\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{k} \exp\frac{c_k}{\varepsilon}\Bigr),
\qquad \varepsilon>0 .
\]
When \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\) we have \(F_\varepsilon\to \max_k c_k\).  
The surrogate is smooth and has a simple gradient:
\[
\frac{\partial F_\varepsilon}{\partial h_i}
   =\sum_{k} w_k\,\frac{\partial c_k}{\partial h_i},
\qquad
w_k = \frac{\exp(c_k/\varepsilon)}{\sum_j\exp(c_j/\varepsilon)} .
\]

**3. Efficient computation of the correlation and its gradient**

*Correlation*  
The vector \(c\) can be obtained by a single discrete convolution:
\[
c = \Delta\; {\rm corr}\bigl(h,\;1-h\bigr) ,
\]
where `np.correlate` (or an FFT‑based convolution) returns the full correlation of length \(2n-1\).

*Gradient of a single shift*  
For a given shift \(k\) the derivative is
\[
\frac{\partial c_k}{\partial h_i}
 =\Delta\bigl((1-h_{i+k})\mathbf{1}_{i+k<n}
               -h_{i-k}\mathbf{1}_{i-k\ge0}\bigr).
\]

*Weighted sum of all shifts*  
Define the weight vector \(w\) (same length as the correlation).  
The weighted gradient can be written using two convolutions:

\[
\begin{aligned}
g_i &= \Delta\Bigl( \underbrace{\sum_k w_k}_{=1}
          -\underbrace{\bigl(w_\text{rev}*h\bigr)_{i+ (n-1)}}_{\displaystyle\sum_k w_k h_{i+k}}
          -\underbrace{\bigl(w*h\bigr)_{i+ (n-1)}}_{\displaystyle\sum_k w_k h_{i-k}}\Bigr) .
\end{aligned}
\]

Here `*` denotes the linear convolution, and \(w_{\rm rev}\) is the reversal of \(w\).
Both convolutions can be performed with `np.convolve` (rapid for \(n\le 300\)) or via FFTs for larger \(n\).

Thus a **single gradient evaluation requires**:
1. one correlation (`np.correlate`) to obtain \(c\);
2. exponentiation and normalisation to obtain \(w\);
3. two convolutions (`np.convolve`) to obtain the weighted sums.

All steps are fully vectorised and run in micro‑seconds for the intended problem sizes.

**4. Projection onto the feasible set**

After each gradient step we must enforce:
\[
0\le h_i\le1,\qquad \sum_i h_i = \tfrac{n}{2}.
\]

A fast *water‑filling* (or *box‑simplex*) projection works as follows:

1. Find a scalar \(\lambda\) such that \(\sum_i \operatorname{clip}(h_i+\lambda,0,1)=\tfrac{n}{2}\).
2. This is a one‑dimensional monotone equation; solve it by a short binary search (≈ 30–40 iterations) because the function is piecewise linear.
3. Return the clipped vector \(\operatorname{clip}(h+\lambda,0,1)\).

The routine is deterministic, cheap, and guarantees feasibility after each update.
