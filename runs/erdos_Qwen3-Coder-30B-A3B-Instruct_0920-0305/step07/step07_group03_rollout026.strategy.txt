**Goal**  
Find a step function \(h:[0,2]\rightarrow[0,1]\) with \(\int_0^2h=1\) that minimises  

\[
C_{5}(h)=\max_{k}\int_0^2 h(x)\bigl(1-h(x+k)\bigr)\,dx .
\]

In the discrete world we work with a vector  

\[
\mathbf h=(h_0,\dots ,h_{N-1})\in[0,1]^N ,\qquad 
\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}h_i = 1\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; \sum_i h_i = N/2 .
\]

The overlap for an integer shift \(\ell\) (the index version of \(k\)) is  

\[
c_\ell(\mathbf h)=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+\ell}\bigr)
\]

where the convention \(h_j=0\) for \(j\notin[0,N-1]\) is used.  
\(C_5(\mathbf h)=\max_{\ell}c_\ell(\mathbf h)\).

The plan below departs radically from the earlier “gradient‑plus‑tiny‑tweak” approach.  
It consists of three tightly coupled stages:

1. **Exact binary optimisation via a Mixed‑Integer Linear Programme (MILP).**  
   The optimal solution among *binary* vectors \(\{0,1\}^N\) is a powerful lower‑bound
   and often already beats the previous record.

2. **Heuristic binary search (simulated‑annealing with lag‑aware moves) that
   quickly produces a high‑quality feasible binary starter for the MILP and also
   serves as a fallback when the MILP cannot finish within the time budget.**

3. **Continuous refinement by a *projected sub‑gradient* method on the true objective
   (the max‑over‑lags), starting from the best binary candidate and allowing
   fractional values.  This exploits the fact that the optimum of the original
   problem may be non‑binary, and it yields the final answer.

All stages respect the box and sum constraints by a single robust *water‑filling*
projection routine (binary‑search on the Lagrange multiplier) and share a fast
FFT‑based correlation routine.

### 1. MILP – binary optimum (exact or near‑exact)

**Variables**

* \(h_i\in\{0,1\}\) – the decision vector (binary step function).  
* For every admissible pair \((i,\ell)\) with \(0\le i\le N-\ell-1\) introduce an
  auxiliary binary variable  

  \[
  p_{i,\ell}=h_i\,h_{i+\ell}\ .
  \]

  The total number of auxilliaries is \(\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\) (≈ 7 200 for \(N=120\)),
  well within the reach of modern open‑source MILP solvers.

**Constraints**

* **Cardinality**: \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}h_i = \frac N2\).  
* **Product linearisation** (for each \((i,\ell)\))  

  \[
  \begin{aligned}
  p_{i,\ell} &\le h_i,\\
  p_{i,\ell} &\le h_{i+\ell},\\
  p_{i,\ell} &\ge h_i+h_{i+\ell}-1 .
  \end{aligned}
  \]

* **Upper bound on each lag**: introduce a continuous variable \(t\) (the
  candidate value of \(C_5\)). For every shift \(\ell\)  

  \[
  \frac{2}{N}\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{N-\ell-1} h_i
      -\sum_{i=0}^{N-\ell-1} p_{i,\ell}\Bigr)\ \le\ t .
  \]

  (The term on the left is exactly \(c_\ell(\mathbf h)\).)

**Objective**  

\[
\min\; t .
\]

**Solver use**

* Build the model once with **cvxpy** using the *CBC* or *SCIP* back‑end.  
* Supply a **warm start**: the binary vector obtained from Stage 2 (see below) is
  used to initialise \(h_i\) and all \(p_{i,\ell}=h_i h_{i+\ell}\).  Warm‑starting
  dramatically reduces branch‑and‑bound effort.  
* Impose a *solver time limit* (e.g. 250 s).  If the solver finishes, we obtain a
  provably optimal binary solution \(\mathbf h^{\text{MILP}}\) and its exact bound
  \(C_5^{\text{MILP}}\).

**Why this is different**  
The previous code never tackled the combinatorial core directly; it only
performed local real‑valued adjustments.  By formulating the problem as an MILP
we explore the whole binary search space globally, guaranteeing that the best
binary pattern is found (or a provably tight lower bound on it), which is a
completely new optimisation paradigm for this task.

### 2. Lag‑aware simulated‑annealing on binary vectors

Even if the MILP does not converge before the global budget expires,
the same annealing routine gives an excellent feasible binary starter for
both the MILP warm‑start and the subsequent continuous refinement.

**Representation** – a binary array with exactly \(N/2\) ones.

**Neighbour generation – three complementary moves**

1. **Swap‑pair** – pick a random ‘1’ index \(i\) and a random ‘0’ index \(j\) and
   exchange them (preserves the sum).  
2. **Block‑swap** – choose two disjoint intervals of equal length \(\ell_{\text{blk}}\)
   (e.g. \(\ell_{\text{blk}}=3\)) and swap the whole blocks; this moves many ones
   simultaneously and creates long‑range changes.  
3. **Mirror‑flip** – select an index \(i\) and set \(h_i:=1-h_i\) while simultaneously
   fixing the sum by flipping another index \(j\) (the swap‑pair is a special case
   of this).

**Cost evaluation** – compute the full correlation by a single FFT
\(\operatorname{corr}= \text{FFT}^{-1}(\text{FFT}(h_{\text{rev}})\cdot\text{FFT}(1-h))\)
and take \(\max(\operatorname{corr})\cdot (2/N)\).

**Annealing schedule**

* **Initial temperature** \(T_0 = 0.1\) (roughly the typical improvement magnitude).  
* **Geometric cooling**: after every \(N_{\text{epoch}}\) proposals set  
  \(T \leftarrow \alpha\,T\) with \(\alpha=0.95\).  
* **Acceptance rule**: a move with cost change \(\Delta\) is accepted with probability  

  \[
  p = \begin{cases}
      1, & \Delta\le 0,\\[4pt]
      \exp(-\Delta/T), & \Delta>0 .
  \end{cases}
  \]

* **Stopping** – continue until the allotted *annealing budget* (≈ 200 s) is exhausted
  or no improvement has been observed for a full cooling cycle.

**Output** – the best binary vector encountered, together with its cost
\(C_5^{\text{SA}}\).

**Why it is materially new**  
The earlier code only performed tiny, untargeted perturbations of a few
coordinates.  Here we
* keep the binary nature throughout,
* deliberately move mass in *blocks* to affect many lags at once,
* explicitly compute and use the *exact* lag that dominates the max,
* employ a classic simulated‑annealing schedule with a warm‑start
  capability for the MILP.

### 3. Continuous refinement – projected sub‑gradient on the max‑lag objective

Even the best binary pattern can often be improved by allowing fractional
values.  We therefore minimise the *non‑smooth* function  

\[
F(\mathbf h)=\max_{\ell} c_\ell(\mathbf h)
\]

by a sub‑gradient method that respects the box and sum constraints.

#### 3.1. Sub‑gradient of a particular lag

For a fixed shift \(\ell\) the (unnormalised) overlap is  

\[
C_\ell(\mathbf h)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+\ell}\bigr) .
\]

Treating out‑of‑range entries of \(\mathbf h\) as zero gives the gradient
componentwise (derivative w.r.t. \(h_k\)):

\[
\frac{\partial C_\ell}{\partial h_k}= 
\underbrace{\bigl(1-h_{k+\ell}\bigr)}_{\text{if }k+\ell<N}
\;-\;
\underbrace{h_{k-\ell}}_{\text{if }k-\ell\ge 0}.
\]

Implementation (vectorised):

* If \(\ell\ge 0\)  
  * `g[:N-ℓ] = 1 - h[ℓ:]`  
  * `g[ℓ:]   -= h[:N-ℓ]`.
* If \(\ell<0\) (let \(d=-\ell\))  
  * `g[d:]   = 1 - h[:N-d]`  
  * `g[:N-d] -= h[d:]`.

This formula is also valid for \(\ell=0\) (gradient is \(1-2h\)), which
allows the algorithm to trade a little self‑overlap for a reduction of the
worst off‑peak lag.

#### 3.2. Sub‑gradient for the max‑operator

Let \(\mathcal L =\{\ell \mid c_\ell(\mathbf h)=F(\mathbf h)\}\) be the set of
max‑achieving lags (typically one or a few).  
A valid sub‑gradient is the average of the individual lag gradients:

\[
g = \frac{1}{|\mathcal L|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal L} \nabla C_\ell(\mathbf h).
\]

#### 3.3. Projected update

1. Choose a step size \(\eta_t\) that decays (e.g. \(\eta_t = \eta_0 / \sqrt{t}\); start
   with \(\eta_0=0.5\)).  
2. Take an unconstrained step: \(\tilde{\mathbf h}= \mathbf h - \eta_t\,g\).  
3. **Projection onto the box & sum**:  
   * First clip \(\tilde{\mathbf h}\) to \([0,1]\).  
   * If the resulting sum equals \(N/2\) we are done.  
   * Otherwise solve  

     \[
     \min_{\mathbf x}\ \|\mathbf x-\tilde{\mathbf h}\|_2
     \quad\text{s.t.}\quad
     0\le x_i\le 1,\ \sum_i x_i = N/2 .
     \]

     The optimal solution is obtained by a simple *water‑filling* (binary‑search
     on a scalar \(\lambda\) such that \(\sum_i\operatorname{clip}(\tilde h_i+\lambda,0,1)=N/2\)).
4. Set \(\mathbf h\gets\) projected vector.

#### 3.4. Acceptance & occasional escape

* If the new cost \(F(\mathbf h_{\text{new}})\) is lower, keep it.  
* If it is higher, still accept with a small probability
  \(\exp\bigl(-(F_{\text{new}}-F_{\text{old}})/\tau_t\bigr)\) where
  \(\tau_t\) is a secondary “exploration temperature” that decays faster
  than \(\eta_t\).  This guards against becoming stuck at a non‑optimal vertex.

#### 3.5. Termination

Run the sub‑gradient loop until the **remaining time** falls below
≈ 30 s or until 10 000 iterations without improvement have been observed.
Keep the best vector seen throughout the whole run.
