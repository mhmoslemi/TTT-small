**1.  Problem reformulation**

The objective can be written as  

\[
C_{5}(h)=\max_{k}\int_{0}^{2}h(x)\bigl(1-h(x+k)\bigr)\,dx
        = \underbrace{\int_{0}^{2}h(x)\,dx}_{=1}
          \;-\;\min_{k}\int_{0}^{2}h(x)\,h(x+k)\,dx .
\]

Hence minimizing `C5` is equivalent to **maximising the minimal self‑overlap**
\(\displaystyle I(k)=\int h(x)h(x+k)\,dx\).  In the discrete world (step‑function with
`n` equally spaced samples, step size `dx=2/n`) we have  

\[
I_{s}(h)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-s}h_{i}\,h_{i+s},\qquad 
C_{s}(h)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-s}h_{i}\;-\;I_{s}(h)
\]

for every integer shift `s∈[-(n‑1), … , n‑1]`.  
The constraints are

* box: \(0\le h_i\le 1\) for all \(i\);
* mass: \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h_i = n/2\)   (because \(\int_0^2 h = 1\)).

Thus we have a **min‑max problem** with a simple feasible set.

**2.  Sub‑gradient descent on the L∞‑objective**

For a fixed `h` let  

\[
c_s(h)=C_{s}(h)  \quad\text{(the correlation for shift }s\text{)} .
\]

The objective is \(F(h)=\max_s c_s(h)\).  Because each \(c_s\) is
differentiable (it is linear minus a quadratic term) the sub‑gradient of
\(F\) at `h` is any convex combination of the gradients of the *worst*
shifts, i.e. the shifts attaining the maximal correlation.

*Gradient of a single shift*  
For a concrete shift `s≥0` the overlapping index set is 
\(I_s=\{0,\dots,n-1-s\}\).  Differentiating  
\(c_s=\sum_{i∈I_s}h_i-\sum_{i∈I_s}h_i\,h_{i+s}\) gives  

\[
\frac{\partial c_s}{\partial h_i}= 
\begin{cases}
1-h_{i+s}, & i\in I_s,\\[4pt]
-\,h_{i-s}, & i\in \{s,\dots ,n-1\},\\[4pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

For a negative shift `s<0` the same formula holds after swapping the
roles of the two indices.  In vectorised form we can compute the whole
gradient of a given worst shift in **O(n)** time.

*Sub‑gradient step*  
Given the set `W` of worst shifts (usually one or two), the sub‑gradient is  

\[
g = \frac{1}{|W|}\sum_{s\in W}\nabla c_s(h).
\]

We then perform a simple projected step  

\[
h^{\text{new}} = \Pi_{\mathcal{F}}\!\bigl( h-\alpha\,g \bigr),
\]

where \(\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}\) is the Euclidean projection onto the feasible
set \(\mathcal{F}=\{h\mid 0\le h_i\le 1,\;\sum h_i=n/2\}\) and \(\alpha>0\) is
the step size.

**3.  Projection onto the capped simplex**

Projecting onto the box \([0,1]^n\) together with a single linear equality
is a classic *capped‑simplex* projection.  It can be solved by a
one‑dimensional bisection on the Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) :

* Define the clipping operator  
  \(\displaystyle \operatorname{clip}_\lambda(v)_i=
     \min\bigl\{1,\max\{0,\,v_i-\lambda\}\bigr\}\).

* The function \(S(\lambda)=\sum_i \operatorname{clip}_\lambda(v)_i\) is
  continuous, strictly decreasing, with \(S(-\infty)=n\) and
  \(S(+\infty)=0\).  The unique \(\lambda^\*\) satisfying
  \(S(\lambda^\*)=n/2\) is found by bisection (≈60 iterations gives
  double‑precision accuracy).

* The projected vector is `x = clip_{λ*}(v)`.

This projection is **O(n log n)** (actually O(n) per bisection step).

    maxc  ← corr.max()

    # 2) sub‑gradient of the max‑correlation
    g ← zero vector
    for each shift s in worst:
         compute gradient of c_s (vectorised O(n)) and add to g
    g ← g / len(worst)

    α ← α0 / sqrt(iteration+1)   (α0≈1.0)

    # 4) projected gradient step
