**Goal** – Find a step‑function `h` on `[0,2]` (discretised to `n` points) with  
`0 ≤ h[i] ≤ 1`, `∑ h[i]·dx = 1` (`dx = 2/n`) that minimises  

\[
C_5(h)=\max_{k\in\{-n+1,\dots,n-1\}}\,\int_0^2 h(x)\bigl(1-h(x+k)\bigr)\,dx
      =dx\;\max_k\;c_k,
\qquad
c_k=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} h_i\,(1-h_{i+k}) .
\]

The problem is non‑convex because of the bilinear term `h_i h_{i+k}`.  
The previous solution used a simple projected gradient on a soft‑max
approximation of the max.  The new plan replaces that with a **continuation
Quasi‑Newton optimisation on an unconstrained parametrisation**, followed by

earlier algorithm.

### 1. Discretisation & Symmetry

* Choose a modest but sufficiently fine grid, e.g. `n = 200` (≈ 0.01 spacing).  
  Finer grids give a tighter bound but increase cost only modestly; `n=200`
  keeps each FFT `O(n log n)` ≈ a few microseconds.

* Enforce the natural symmetry `h[i] = h[n‑1‑i]`.  
  After each iteration we replace `h` by `(h + h[::-1])/2` and re‑normalise.
  This halves the effective dimension and focuses the search on symmetric
  candidates (the optimal solution is expected to be symmetric).

* The exact area constraint is `target_sum = n/2` (since `dx·target_sum = 1`).

---

### 2. Unconstrained parametrisation – sigmoid variables

Introduce free variables `z_i ∈ ℝ` and define

\[
h_i = \sigma(z_i)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z_i}} \in (0,1) .
\]

Box constraints are satisfied automatically.  
The equality constraint on the sum is enforced *softly* by a quadratic
penalty term

\[
P_\lambda(z)=\lambda\bigl(\sum_i \sigma(z_i)-\text{target\_sum}\bigr)^2 .
\]

`λ` will be increased during the continuation so that the final
solution respects the area constraint to machine precision.

### 3. Smooth approximation of the max

For a fixed smoothing parameter `α>0` define the log‑sum‑exp surrogate

\[
F_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{k}e^{\alpha\,c_k(z)}\Bigr),
\qquad
c_k(z)=\sum_i \sigma(z_i)-\sum_i\sigma(z_i)\,\sigma(z_{i+k}) .
\]

`F_α` is a smooth, convex‑in‑the‑large approximation of the max; as
`α→∞`, `F_α → max_k c_k`.  The overall objective for a given `(α,λ)` is

\[
\Phi_{\alpha,\lambda}(z)=F_{\alpha}(z)+P_\lambda(z).
\]

Only the **raw** correlation values `c_k` (without the factor `dx`) are
required for the gradient – the multiplication by `dx` at the end does not
affect the optimisation direction.

### 4. Analytic gradients (fully vectorised)

*Derivative of the sigmoid*: `σ'(z)=σ(z)(1-σ(z))`.

*Derivative of a correlation component*:

\[
\frac{\partial c_k}{\partial h_i}= 1 - h_{i+k} - h_{i-k},
\qquad
\frac{\partial c_k}{\partial z_i}= (1-h_{i+k}-h_{i-k})\,\sigma'(z_i),
\]
where out‑of‑range indices are ignored.

*Derivative of the log‑sum‑exp*: define soft‑max weights

\[
w_k = \frac{e^{\alpha c_k}}{\sum_j e^{\alpha c_j}} .
\]

Then  

\[
\nabla_z F_{\alpha} = \sum_k w_k\,\nabla_z c_k .
\]

*Derivative of the penalty*:

\[
\nabla_z P_\lambda = 2\lambda\bigl(\sum_i h_i-\text{target\_sum}\bigr)
                    \,\sigma'(z) .
\]

All the above operations are expressed with NumPy arrays and **FFT‑based
convolutions** to compute the vector of `c_k` and the sums
`h_{i+k}+h_{i-k}` efficiently (`O(n log n)`).  No explicit loops over shifts
or indices are needed.

### 5. Quasi‑Newton optimisation (L‑BFGS‑B)

SciPy’s `optimize.minimize` with method `'L-BFGS-B'` will be used on the
vector `z`.  The method already stores an inverse‑Hessian approximation,

difference from simple gradient descent.

  3. **Binary block** – half the entries set to 1, the rest 0, then transformed to `z`.  

  Each is passed through `σ⁻¹` to obtain `z₀`.

* **Continuation schedule** – optimisation proceeds in stages:

### 7. Local stochastic fine‑tuning (post‑processing)

The quasi‑Newton result is extremely close to a local optimum, but a few
tiny adjustments can still lower the true max correlation:

* **Pairwise mass transfer** – repeatedly pick indices `i` and `j` (with

* **Acceptance rule** – accept the move iff the new `C₅` is lower; otherwise
  reject.  Perform this for a fixed number of trials (e.g. 10 000) or until
  the time budget is exhausted.
* Because each trial only needs a single `np.correlate` call, the
  post‑processing fits comfortably in the remaining budget.
