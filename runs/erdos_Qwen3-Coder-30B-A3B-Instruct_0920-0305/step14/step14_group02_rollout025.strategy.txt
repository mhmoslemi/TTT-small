### 1.  Problem restatement (discrete version)

*Grid:* choose an integer `N` (≈ 120‑150) and let `dx = 2/N`.  
*Variables:* `h = (h_0,…,h_{N-1})` with `0 ≤ h_i ≤ 1`.  
*Sum constraint:* `∑_{i} h_i = N/2` (because the integral must be 1).  

For a shift `s ∈ {-(N-1),…,N-1}` define the overlap  

\[
c_s(h)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1-s} h_i\,(1-h_{i+s}) \qquad(s\ge 0)
\]

(and the analogous expression for negative `s`).  
The quantity to minimise is  

\[
C_5(h)=dx\;\max_{s} c_s(h).
\tag{1}
\]

### 2.  A smooth surrogate for the max

Directly minimising (1) is nondifferentiable.  Replace the max by a
log‑sum‑exp (soft‑max) with a temperature `T>0`:

\[
F_T(h)=dx\;T\;\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{s} \exp\!\bigl(c_s(h)/T\bigr)\Bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

When `T → 0` we have `F_T(h) → C_5(h)`.  For any positive `T`,
`F_T` is smooth and its gradient can be written in closed form.

*Gradient of a single shift.*  
For a fixed shift `s` the derivative of `c_s` follows from (1):

\[
\bigl[\nabla c_s(h)\bigr]_j=
\begin{cases}
1-h_{j+s} & \text{if }0\le j\le N-1-s,\\[1mm]
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
-
\begin{cases}
h_{j-s}   & \text{if }s\le j\le N-1,\\[1mm]
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\tag{3}
\]

*Gradient of the surrogate.*  
Let  

\[
w_s = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(c_s(h)/T\bigr)}
            {\sum_{t}\exp\!\bigl(c_t(h)/T\bigr)} .
\]

Then  

\[
\nabla F_T(h)=dx\;\sum_{s} w_s\,\nabla c_s(h).
\tag{4}
\]

Because `w_s` is heavily concentrated on the few largest shifts,
we evaluate (4) only on the **top‑K** shifts (e.g. `K=12`);
the neglected terms have negligible weight and the cost stays linear in `N`.

### 3.  Deterministic quasi‑Newton optimiser on the smooth problem

We solve  

\[
\min_{h\in[0,1]^N,\; \sum h=N/2} F_T(h)
\tag{5}
\]

by a **projected L‑BFGS‑B** (limited‑memory BFGS with box bounds) wrapped
inside a simple equality‑projection step:

1. **Gradient supply** – supply to L‑BFGS the vector from (4).  
2. **Box handling** – L‑BFGS‑B respects the `0–1` bounds automatically.  
3. **Equality handling** – after each L‑BFGS update we project the trial
   point onto the *capped simplex*  
   \(\{h\in[0,1]^N:\sum h=N/2\}\) using a **water‑filling bisection**:
   - add a scalar `λ` to every component,
   - clamp to `[0,1]`,
   - adjust `λ` by bisection until the sum equals `N/2`.
   This projection is `O(N log ε)` and is performed only once per
   L‑BFGS iteration.

The optimiser is therefore deterministic (no random mutations) and
exploits curvature information, which is a substantial change from the
previous evolutionary and coordinate‑descent schemes.

   affine functions `c_s(δ) = a_s·δ + b_s`.  
3. The objective `max_s c_s(δ)` is the upper envelope of those lines.
   Its minimum over the feasible interval occurs either at an endpoint
   or at an intersection of two lines.  All candidate points are obtained
   by solving `a_s·δ + b_s = a_t·δ + b_t` for pairs of shifts with
   different slopes.  Since `N` is modest, enumerating all `O(N²)` pairs
   is cheap (a few milliseconds).  
4. Evaluate `max_s c_s(δ)` at every candidate and pick the `δ` with the
   smallest value.  If the improvement is > 1e‑7, perform the exchange,
   update `ĥ` and recompute `c`.  

We sweep the index pairs in a **random order** and repeat the whole sweep
until a full pass makes no improvement.  Because each exchange is
*globally optimal* for its pair, this stage converges rapidly and can
reduce `C_5` by 1–2·10⁻⁴ compared to the L‑BFGS solution.

Optionally, to escape shallow plateaus we add a tiny *simulated‑annealing*
probability: if a candidate exchange is slightly worse (by < 5·10⁻⁵) we
accept it with probability `exp(−Δ/τ)` and decay `τ` linearly to zero over
the polishing phase.

### 8.  Optional convex relaxation for pruning

Before launching a restart we compute a **linear relaxation** of the
max‑over‐shifts problem using **McCormick envelopes** for the bilinear
terms `h_i·h_{i+s}`:

- Introduce auxiliary variables `w_{i,s}`.
- Impose
  ```
  w_{i,s} ≥ 0,
  w_{i,s} ≤ h_i,
  w_{i,s} ≤ h_{i+s},
  w_{i,s} ≥ h_i + h_{i+s} - 1.
  ```
- The overlap for shift `s` becomes `∑ h_i - ∑ w_{i,s}` (linear).
- Add a variable `t` and enforce `∑ h_i - ∑ w_{i,s} ≤ t` for all `s`.
- Minimise `t` subject to the box and sum constraints.

The resulting linear program (≈ N² constraints, < 20 000 for `N=120`) is
tiny for modern LP solvers (ECOS, SCS, or the open‑source CBC).  Solving it
gives a **lower bound** on the true `C_5`.  If the bound for a particular
restart exceeds the best feasible value already found, we discard that
restart without spending L‑BFGS iterations.  This cheap pruning saves
time and is entirely different from the stochastic methods used before.

### 9.  Failure‑mode analysis & mitigations

| Failure mode | Reason & Symptom | Mitigation |
|--------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Stagnation because `T` becomes too small too early** | Gradients become almost zero; L‑BFGS stalls with a sub‑optimal `C_5`. | Use a *gentle* decay (`τ_T≈0.998`) and monitor the improvement; if the surrogate gradient norm falls below a threshold *before* `T` is tiny, pause decay for a few iterations. |
| **Projection bottleneck** | Bisection on `λ` iterates many times each L‑BFGS step, wasting time. | Pre‑compute the sorted values of `h` once per iteration and perform the classic “water‑filling” in O(N) instead of O(N log ε). |
| **Numerical overflow in `exp(c_s/T)`** | Very large `c_s/T` for tiny `T`. | Subtract the maximum correlation before exponentiation (`exp((c - max(c))/T)`). |
| **Non‑convexity of the original problem causing L‑BFGS to wander into a bad basin** | Final `C_5` still > 0.381 after many restarts. | Increase the **number of restarts**, ensure a *diverse* seed set (random, symmetric, triangular), and use the **relaxation based pruning** to focus effort on promising regions. |
| **Pairwise exchange enumeration too costly** | O(N²) candidate `δ` values per pair. | Limit the exhaustive line‑search to the **current worst shift(s)** only (e.g. top‑5), which reduces the candidate set dramatically without sacrificing quality. |
| **Time overflow** | The budget (600 s) is exceeded before polishing finishes. | Split the total budget into earmarked portions: 70 % for the L‑BFGS continuation (including all restarts), 20 % for the LP relaxation & pruning, 10 % for the pairwise exchange.  At each stage check `time.time()` and abort gracefully, returning the best vector found so far. |

### 10.  End‑to‑end workflow (pseudo‑timeline)

1. **Initialisation** (`t = 0`).  
   - Set `N = 120` (or 150 if early time allows).  
   - Compute `dx = 2/N`, `target = N/2`.  
   - Start the timer.

2. **Coarse optimisation (N=60)** – ≤ 30 s.  
   - Build the triangular seed, project, run L‑BFGS with continuation.  
   - Store coarse optimum `h_coarse`.

3. **Interpolation to fine grid** – ≤ 5 s.  
   - Linear interpolation of `h_coarse` to `N` points, project.

4. **Restart loop** – consume ≈ 0.55 × budget.  
   For each restart (until time budget or max‑restart count):
   - Generate a seed (random, symmetric random, triangular, etc.).
   - Run L‑BFGS‑with‑continuation (max ≈ 2000 iterations; early‑stop if
     time exhausted).  
   - After convergence evaluate the *exact* `C_5` via FFT.  
   - If a **linear‑relaxation lower bound** (LP) for this seed exceeds the
     current best feasible `C_5`, discard the restart immediately.  
   - Keep the best feasible `(h, C_5)` overall.

5. **Exact pairwise polishing** – remaining ≈ 0.15 × budget.  
   - Starting from the best `h`, perform the analytical pairwise exchange
     sweep until no improvement or time is up.  
   - Optionally sprinkle a few simulated‑annealing uphill moves with a
     linearly decaying temperature.

6. **Final sanity projection & evaluation** – ≤ 5 s.  
   - One final projection onto the capped simplex to clean numeric drift.  
   - Compute the exact `C_5` with a full FFT correlation (the value to be
     returned).

7. **Return** `(h.tolist(), float(C_5), N)`.

### 11.  Why this is materially different

* **Deterministic quasi‑Newton optimisation** (projected L‑BFGS) replaces
  the stochastic differential‑evolution and simple coordinate descent of the
  earlier attempts.
* **Log‑sum‑exp temperature continuation** provides a smooth, analytically
  differentiable surrogate for the max, enabling the use of curvature‑aware
  methods.
* **Exact pairwise exchange with analytic line‑search** improves the final
  bound deterministically; it is not a random perturbation but a global
  optimum for each two‑coordinate move.
* **Coarse‑to‑fine multiscale boot‑strapping** and **symmetry enforcement**
  are new hierarchical tricks not present before.
* **Linear‑relaxation pruning via McCormick envelopes** supplies a cheap
  lower bound that guides the restart policy, an altogether different
  global‑search paradigm.

By combining these ingredients we obtain a robust, fast, and provably
monotone descent scheme that should push the empirical upper bound on the
Erdős constant below the current record (target ≤ 0.3807) within the 600‑second
budget.
