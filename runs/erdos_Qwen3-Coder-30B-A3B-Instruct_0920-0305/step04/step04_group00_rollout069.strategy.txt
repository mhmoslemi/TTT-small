**1.  Core mathematical observation**  
For a discretised step function \(h\in[0,1]^n\) with \(\sum_i h_i\,dx=1\) (i.e. \(\sum_i h_i=n/2\) because \(dx=2/n\)), the quantity to minimise is  

\[
C_5=\max_{k\in\{-(n-1),\dots ,n-1\}} \; \Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} h_i\,(1-h_{i+k})\Bigr)dx .
\]

Since \(\sum_i h_i = n/2\) is constant,  

\[
C_5=1-\frac{2}{n}\;\min_{k}\;\sum_{i}h_i\,h_{i+k}\, .
\]

Hence we must **maximise the minimal self‑overlap** \(\sum_i h_i h_{i+k}\) over all admissible integer shifts \(k\). Large values of the overlap are obtained when the mass of \(h\) is concentrated in the centre of the interval and decays towards the boundaries, because for large shifts only the edge values contribute to the overlap loss.

**2.  Reduce the search space – a low‑dimensional, symmetric, monotone family**  

*Symmetry*: Any optimal \(h\) can be symmetrised about the centre \(x=1\) without worsening the objective.  
*Monotonicity*: Replacing a point farther from the centre by a larger value while moving the same mass inward never decreases the minimal overlap, so an optimal shape can be taken non‑increasing as distance from the centre grows.

Define a half‑interval \([0,1]\). Choose a small integer \(K\) (e.g. 4 or 5) and introduce break‑points  

\[
0=x_0<x_1<\dots <x_K=1 .
\]

Associate non‑increasing heights  

\[
1=h_0\ge h_1\ge\dots\ge h_K\ge0 .
\]

The full vector of length \(n\) is obtained by **linear interpolation** of \((x_j,h_j)\) on the half‑interval, then mirroring the values to the other half \([1,2]\). This yields a piecewise‑linear, symmetric, decreasing function.

After interpolation we obtain a raw vector \(\tilde h\). Enforce the integral constraint by a **global scaling**  

\[
\lambda=\frac{n/2}{\sum_i \tilde h_i}\,,\qquad h_i=\lambda\,\tilde h_i .
\]

If any entry exceeds 1 after scaling, clip it to 1 and repeat the scaling until the sum equals \(n/2\) (a few iterations converge).  
Thus the optimisation variables are the unordered heights \(\{u_j\}_{j=0}^K\in[0,1]\); during decoding we sort them decreasingly to guarantee monotonicity.

**4.  Fine‑tuning the full‑resolution vector with a smooth surrogate**  

The max‑operator in the definition of \(C_5\) is non‑differentiable. Replace it by a **log‑sum‑exp smooth approximation**:

\[
L_{\beta}(h)=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\Bigl(\sum_{k}e^{\beta f_k(h)}\Bigr),
\qquad
f_k(h)=\sum_i h_i\,(1-h_{i+k})\,dx .
\]

With \(\beta\in[15,25]\) the surrogate closely follows the true maximum while remaining smooth.

*Gradient of a single shift*:
\[
\frac{\partial f_k}{\partial h_j}=dx\bigl(1-h_{j+k}-h_{j-k}\bigr),
\]
where indices outside \([0,n-1]\) are ignored (treated as zero).  

*Gradient of the surrogate*:
\[
\nabla L_{\beta}(h)=\sum_k w_k \,\nabla f_k,
\qquad
w_k=\frac{e^{\beta f_k}}{\sum_{\ell}e^{\beta f_{\ell}}}.
\]

Thus the gradient is a convex combination of the per‑shift gradients and can be computed in \(\mathcal O(n^2)\) time (the same cost as the correlation).

**5.  Projection onto the feasible set**  

After a gradient step we must respect  

\[
0\le h_i\le 1,\qquad \sum_i h_i\,dx = 1 .
\]

This is the projection onto the intersection of a box and a simplex. It can be performed efficiently by a **water‑filling (dual) algorithm**:

1. Find \(\lambda\) such that the vector \(p_i=\operatorname{clip}(h_i-\lambda,0,1)\) satisfies \(\sum_i p_i = n/2\).  
2. Solve for \(\lambda\) with a binary search on a bounded interval \([-1,1]\) (the range is known because the sum of the unclipped vector is at most \(n\) and at least \(0\)).  
3. Return the clipped vector \(p\) as the projected point.
