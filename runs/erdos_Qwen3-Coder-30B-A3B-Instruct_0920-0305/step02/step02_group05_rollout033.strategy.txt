**1. Mathematical reformulation**

The quantity to minimise is  

\[
C_{5}(h)=\max_{k\in[-2,2]} \int_{[0,2]\cap[ -k, 2-k]}h(x)\bigl(1-h(x+k)\bigr)\;dx .
\]

With a uniform discretisation of the interval \([0,2]\) into \(n\) points (step \(\Delta x = 2/n\)) this becomes  

\[
C_{5}(h)=\Delta x\;\max_{m\in\{-n+1,\dots ,n-1\}}
\Bigl[\sum_{i\in I_m}h_i\;-\;\sum_{i\in I_m}h_i\,h_{i+m}\Bigr],
\]

where \(I_m=\{i\mid 0\le i\le n-1-m\}\) for a non‑negative shift \(m\) (the case \(m<0\) is symmetric).  
Denote  

\[
A_m(h)=\sum_{i\in I_m}\bigl(h_i-h_i h_{i+m}\bigr) .
\]

Thus \(C_{5}(h)=\Delta x\;\max_{m}A_m(h)\).

The feasible set is

* box constraints \(0\le h_i\le 1\);
* linear equality \(\sum_i h_i = n/2\) (because \(\int_0^2 h =1\)).

The objective is a **max of a family of smooth functions** \(A_m\); each \(A_m\) has a simple analytic gradient.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
**2. Sub‑gradient / projected‑gradient scheme for the min‑max problem**

*Treat the max as a nondifferentiable function and use a sub‑gradient descent on it.*

At a point \(h\) let  

\[
\mathcal M = \{ m\;|\;A_m(h)=\max_{k}A_k(h)\}
\]

be the set of “active” shifts (normally one or a few).  
A sub‑gradient of \(\max_m A_m\) is any convex combination of the gradients \(\nabla A_m\) for \(m\in\mathcal M\).  
We will simply **average** the gradients of all active shifts:

\[
g(h)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal M|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal M}\nabla A_m(h).
\]

*Gradient of a single shift* \(m\ge 0\) (the case \(m<0\) is symmetric):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_i A_m(h) &=
\begin{cases}
1-h_{i+m}, & i\in I_m,\\[4pt]
-\,h_{i-m}, & i-m\in I_m\;(i\ge m),\\[4pt]
0, &\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]

In vectorised form (for \(m\ge 0\)):

```
g[i]        += 1 - h[i+m]   for i = 0 … n-m-1
g[i+m]      -= h[i]        for i = 0 … n-m-1
```

and multiply the whole vector by the factor \(\Delta x\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
**3. Projection onto the feasible set**

After a gradient step we must enforce:
* 0 ≤ h ≤ 1,
* \(\sum_i h_i = n/2\).

The projection onto the intersection of a box and an affine hyperplane is a **water‑filling** problem.  
Algorithm (vectorised, O(n)) :

1. Clip:  `w = np.clip(v,0,1)`.
2. Compute the deficit/excess: `δ = target - w.sum()`.
3. While `|δ|` is larger than a tiny tolerance:
   * If `δ>0` (need to increase the sum) take the free indices where `w<1`.  
     Add `δ/|free|` to all free entries, then clip again.
   * If `δ<0` (need to decrease) take the free indices where `w>0`.  
     Subtract `|δ|/|free|` from them and clip again.
   * Recompute `δ`.  

Because each iteration removes at least one variable from the free set, the loop finishes in at most `n` steps.

**4. Enforcing symmetry (optional but powerful)**

The problem is invariant under the reflection `x → 2‑x`.  
If we restrict to symmetric functions, the search space halves and the optimal value cannot increase.  
Implementation: keep only the first `⌈n/2⌉` variables, mirror them to obtain the full vector, then apply the projection only on the independent half (the water‑filling is performed on the full vector after mirroring).

The symmetry step can be applied after every projection:

```
h = (h + h[::-1]) / 2
```

followed by a second projection to restore the exact sum.
