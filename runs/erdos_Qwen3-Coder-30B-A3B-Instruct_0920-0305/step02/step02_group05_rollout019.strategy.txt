### 1. Analytic reformulation

For a sampled vector `h∈ℝⁿ` (`dx=2/n`) let  

\[
A_m(h)=dx\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-|m|} h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+|m|}\bigr)\quad (m=-n+1,\dots,n-1).
\]

Then  

\[
C_5(h)=\max_m A_m(h)=1-\min_m R_m(h),\qquad
R_m(h)=dx\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-|m|} h_i\,h_{i+|m|} .
\]

`R_m` is exactly the discrete autocorrelation of `h` on the overlapping part of the shift `m`.  
If we embed `h` into a **periodic** function of period `2` (zero‑padding outside the interval), the autocorrelation becomes a circular convolution. The discrete Fourier transform diagonalises the convolution:

\[
R_m(h)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\bigl|\hat h_\ell\bigr|^2\,
      \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\ell m}{n}\Bigr),
\qquad 
\hat h =\mathcal F(h).
\]

Thus the whole family `{R_m}` is a linear combination of the **spectral power** `|ĥ_ℓ|²`.  Maximising the *minimum* correlation amounts to shaping the spectrum so that the cosine series stays as large as possible for every `m`.

---

### 2. Low‑dimensional spectral parametrisation

We restrict `h` to a **real, even cosine series** (the optimal solution must be symmetric w.r.t. the centre `x=1`):

\[
h(x)\;=\; \frac12\;+\;\sum_{p=1}^{P} a_p\;\cos\!\bigl(p\pi x\bigr),\qquad x\in[0,2].
\]

*Why this basis?*  
* The constant term `½` guarantees the integral condition (average = ½).  
* Even cosine terms automatically enforce `h(x)=h(2-x)`, halving the number of independent variables and guaranteeing that the worst shift will be symmetric.  
* With `P≈8–12` we obtain a very flexible shape while keeping the optimisation problem tiny (≈10 variables).

For a given coefficient vector `a = (a₁,…,a_P)` we obtain the sampled vector `h` by evaluating the series on the uniform grid `x_i = i·dx`.  
All subsequent quantities (`A_m`, `R_m`) can be computed **exactly** from `a` using the FFT:

1. Build the full periodic vector `h` (length `n`).  
2. Compute its FFT once (`ĥ = FFT(h)`).  
3. Obtain `R_m` for all lags from the inverse FFT of `|ĥ|²`.  
4. Recover `A_m = dx·∑_{i∈I_m} h_i - R_m` (the first term is just the overlap length `dx·|I_m|` multiplied by the local mean of `h`; it can be computed from the cumulative sum of `h`).

All of this is `O(n log n)` and extremely cheap because `n` can be taken as large as 512 without affecting the optimisation cost (the heavy work is the FFT, not a high‑dimensional search).

### 3. Smoothed max‑objective (deterministic annealing)

The raw objective `C_5 = max_m A_m` is non‑smooth.  We replace it by a **log‑sum‑exp** surrogate:

\[
F_\tau(a)=\tau\;\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{m} \exp\!\bigl(A_m(a)/\tau\bigr)\Bigr),
\]

where `τ>0` is a temperature parameter.  
*Properties* – as `τ→0` the surrogate converges pointwise to `max_m A_m`; for large `τ` the landscape is smooth and convex‑like, which aids global exploration.

We embed the box constraints (`0≤h_i≤1`) via a **quadratic penalty** evaluated on the sampled grid:

\[
P(a)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Bigl(\max\{0,-h_i(a)\}^2
                         +\max\{0,\,h_i(a)-1\}^2\Bigr).
\]

The total objective for a given temperature is

\[
\Phi_\tau(a)=F_\tau(a)+\lambda\,P(a),
\]

with a penalty weight `λ` chosen large enough (e.g. `λ=10⁴`) to keep the iterates inside the box up to machine precision.

### 4. Optimisation in coefficient space

**Algorithmic skeleton**

1. **Choose parameters** – `n = 256` (grid for evaluating `h`), `P = 10` (cosine modes), temperature schedule `τ₀ > τ₁ > … > τ_L → 0` (geometric decay, e.g. `τ_k = τ₀·ρ^k` with `ρ≈0.5`).

2. **Initialisation** – start from a random coefficient vector `a` with small amplitude (e.g. normal(0,0.05)).  This automatically respects the integral condition because the constant term is fixed at ½.  Optionally, seed one run with the **interpolated** `initial_h_values` (fit the first `P` cosine coefficients by least squares).

3. **Inner optimisation** – for each temperature `τ_k` run a **gradient‑based method** (Adam or L‑BFGS) on `Φ_{τ_k}`:
   * Compute `h(a)` on the grid, its FFT, the autocorrelation, the array `A_m`.  
   * Compute the soft‑max weights `w_m = exp(A_m/τ)/∑exp(A_m/τ)`.  
   * Derivative of `F_τ` w.r.t. `h` is simply the weighted sum of the sub‑gradients of `A_m` (the gradient of each `A_m` is known analytically from the shift formulas, see Section 1).  
   * Chain‑rule back‑propagates to the coefficients `a` (the derivative of `h_i` w.r.t. `a_p` is `cos(pπx_i)`).  
   * Add the penalty gradient (`∂P/∂h_i = 2·max(0,-h_i)·(-1) + 2·max(0,h_i-1)·1`).  

   Because the whole pipeline uses only matrix‑vector products and FFTs, a single gradient evaluation costs ≈0.001 s for `n=256`.  Running 2000–5000 iterations per temperature comfortably fits the 600 s budget.

4. **Continuation** – after the optimizer converges for the current `τ_k`, decrease temperature to `τ_{k+1}` and **warm‑start** from the last coefficient vector.  As `τ` shrinks the solution automatically sharpens the “max‑lag” and the penalty forces the sampled values into the unit interval.

5. **Projection onto the integral constraint** – the constant term of the cosine series guarantees that `∫₀²h=1` exactly, so no extra projection is needed.  The box penalty ensures `h∈[0,1]`.  If after a temperature step a few samples violate the bounds by more than 10⁻⁸, we simply **clip** those values and perform a single Newton step on the coefficients to restore the cosine representation (solve a small linear system for the correction).

6. **Multi‑start strategy** – repeat the whole continuation from several random seeds (e.g. 8 seeds).  The cheapest seeds finish early; the best final `a` among all runs is retained as `a*`.

### 5. From coefficients back to a feasible step‑function

The optimal coefficient vector `a*` yields a smooth `h*(x)`.  To produce the required *step* representation:

1. Sample `h*` on the grid `x_i = i·dx`.  
2. Apply the **exact projection** onto the feasible polytope `{h∈[0,1]^n, ∑h_i=n/2}` using the water‑filling algorithm (Section 2 of the analysis).  This step guarantees that the final discrete vector respects the integral **exactly**, eliminating any tiny drift incurred by rounding.  

The projection is cheap (`O(n)`) and does not change the shape dramatically because `h*` already respects the bounds.

Optionally, perform a **few iterations of a projected sub‑gradient descent** (the method from Section 1 of the analysis) **starting from this projected vector**.  This fine‑tunes the solution on the original `h`‑space and typically squeezes a few extra hundredths off the final `C_5`.
