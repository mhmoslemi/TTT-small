### 1.  Exact binary formulation (MILP)

For a binary step‑function (the values are 0 or 1) the overlap can be written as  

\[
M_k = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1-k} \bigl(x_i - x_i x_{i+k}\bigr)    \qquad(k>0)
\]

and analogously for negative shifts  

\[
M_{-k}= \sum_{i=0}^{N-1-k} \bigl(x_{i+k} - x_i x_{i+k}\bigr).
\]

The term \(x_i x_{i+k}\) is quadratic, but with binary variables it can be linearised by an auxiliary binary variable  

\[
y_{i,k}=x_i x_{i+k},\qquad
y_{i,k}\le x_i,\;y_{i,k}\le x_{i+k},\;
y_{i,k}\ge x_i+x_{i+k}-1 .
\]

*Variables*  

* \(x_i\in\{0,1\}\) for \(i=0,\dots,N-1\) (the step‑function).  
* \(y_{i,k}\in\{0,1\}\) for every ordered pair \((i,k)\) with \(k\ge1\) and \(i+k<N\).  
* a real variable \(t\ge0\) that bounds the worst overlap.

*Constraints*  

1. **Mass constraint** – exactly half the points are 1: \(\sum_i x_i = N/2\).  
2. **Linearisation** – the three inequalities for each \(y_{i,k}\).  
3. **Overlap bounds** – for every shift \(k=1,\dots,N-1\)   
   \[
   \underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1-k}x_i}_{S_k^{+}}-
   \underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1-k}y_{i,k}}_{A_k}\;\le\;t,
   \qquad
   \underbrace{\sum_{i=k}^{N-1}x_i}_{S_k^{-}}-
   \underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1-k}y_{i,k}}_{A_k}\;\le\;t.
   \]

These are linear in the decision variables; the objective is simply **minimise t**.  The optimal value satisfies  

\[
C_5(N)= t^*\,\frac{2}{N}.
\]

*Symmetry breaking* – to prune the search space we fix the first entry, e.g. \(x_0=0\).  Complementing every bit (replace 0↔1) leaves the objective unchanged, so this does not affect optimality.

### 2.  Continuous refinement (smooth‑max gradient descent)

Binary solutions are often close to optimal, but allowing fractional values can sometimes lower the maximal overlap a little further.

*Variables* – a real vector \(h\in[0,1]^N\) with the same mass constraint \(\sum h_i = N/2\).

*Smoothed objective* – replace the nondifferentiable `max` by a log‑sum‑exp (soft‑max) with a temperature \(\alpha>0\):

\[
F_\alpha(h)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\Bigl(\sum_{k=-N+1}^{N-1}\exp\bigl(\alpha\,\underbrace{\sum_i h_i(1-h_{i+k})}_{=:\,g_k(h)}\bigr)\Bigr).
\]

When \(\alpha\) is large (e.g. 50–200) \(F_\alpha\) is a very tight upper bound on \(\max_k g_k\).  Its gradient is analytic:

\[
\frac{\partial F_\alpha}{\partial h_j}= 
\sum_k w_k\Bigl(\mathbf{1}_{j\le N-1-k}\bigl(1-h_{j+k}\bigr)
-\mathbf{1}_{j\ge k}h_{j-k}\Bigr),
\qquad
w_k=\frac{e^{\alpha g_k}}{\sum_\ell e^{\alpha g_\ell}} .
\]

*Projected gradient scheme*  

1. Initialise \(h\) with the binary optimum from Stage 1 (or with a random balanced vector if Stage 1 failed).  
2. Loop until the time budget is exhausted or progress stalls:  
   a. Compute all \(g_k(h)\) via a single correlation (`np.correlate`).  
   b. Compute the soft‑max weights \(w_k\) and the gradient using the formula above (vectorised NumPy operations).  
   c. Take a step \(h \leftarrow h - \eta\,\nabla F_\alpha(h)\) with a modest stepsize \(\eta\) (e.g. start at 0.1 and decay).  
   d. **Projection** – first clip to \([0,1]\), then enforce the mass constraint by shifting all coordinates by the same amount:  
      \[
      h \leftarrow h + \frac{(N/2)-\sum h_i}{N}\;\mathbf{1},
      \]  
      followed by a second clip to keep the values in \([0,1]\).  This is the Euclidean projection onto the intersection of the hyper‑cube and the affine simplex.  
   e. Evaluate the true max‑overlap (`evaluate_h`) and keep the best‑ever vector.  
   f. Occasionally increase \(\alpha\) (e.g. from 50 → 200) to tighten the approximation as we approach a local optimum.  

Because the gradient is inexpensive (≈ O(N²) but with N≤80 it is negligible), we can afford several thousand iterations in the remaining time.

### 3.  Fast stochastic search (simulated‑annealing / hill‑climbing)
