### 1.  Problem recap & useful reformulation
For a discrete step‑function `h` (length `N`, `dx = 2/N`) we have  

\[
C_5(h)=\max_{k}\int_0^2 h(x)\bigl(1-h(x+k)\bigr)dx 
      = 1-\min_{k}\int_0^2 h(x)h(x+k)dx .
\]

Thus **minimising** `C5` is equivalent to **maximising** the smallest autocorrelation value  

\[
a_k(h)=\int_0^2 h(x)h(x+k)dx .
\]

For the discrete vector `h` the (linear, non‑circular) autocorrelation is  

\[
a_s = dx\sum_{i=0}^{N-1-s} h_i\,h_{i+s},
\qquad s=-(N-1),\dots ,N-1 .
\]

Hence we want to maximise  

\[
m(h)=\min_s a_s .
\]

The constraints are  

* box: \(0\le h_i\le 1\)  
* linear: \(\sum_i h_i = N/2\)    (because \(\int_0^2 h=1\)).

The min‑operator makes the problem non‑smooth.  
We replace it by a smooth lower‑bound using a **soft‑min** (log‑sum‑exp) with a temperature parameter `α>0`:

\[
\widetilde m_\alpha(h)= -\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\!\Bigl(\sum_s e^{-\alpha a_s}\Bigr).
\]

When `α→∞` the soft‑min converges to the true minimum.  
Our optimisation objective becomes  

\[
f_\alpha(h) = 1- \widetilde m_\alpha(h) \; \approx\; C_5(h) .
\]

The function `f_α` is **smooth** (infinitely differentiable) and we can compute its gradient analytically.

### 2.  Gradient of the smoothed objective
Define the weight vector  

\[
w_s = \frac{e^{-\alpha a_s}}{\sum_t e^{-\alpha a_t}}   \qquad (w_s\ge 0,\; \sum w_s=1).
\]

Then  

\[
\frac{\partial \widetilde m_\alpha}{\partial a_s}= -w_s .
\]

For a fixed shift `s`

\[
a_s = dx\sum_i h_i h_{i+s}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial a_s}{\partial h_k}=dx\bigl(h_{k+s}+h_{k-s}\bigr),
\]

where out‑of‑range indices are interpreted as zero.  
Putting the chain rule together,

\[
\frac{\partial f_\alpha}{\partial h_k}
   = -\frac{\partial \widetilde m_\alpha}{\partial h_k}
   =  \sum_s w_s\,dx\bigl(h_{k+s}+h_{k-s}\bigr) .
\]

Thus the **gradient vector** is

\[
g = dx\Bigl[\,\underbrace{w * h}_{\text{shift }\!+}\;+\;
            \underbrace{w * h}_{\text{shift }\!-}\,\Bigr],
\]

where `*` denotes a *linear* convolution (zero‑padded) of the weight sequence `w` with `h`.  
Explicitly it can be computed with a simple loop over the `2N-1` shifts; with `N≤300` this is negligible compared with FFT based autocorrelation.

### 3.  Projection onto the feasible set
After an **unconstrained** gradient step `h←h‑η g` we must bring the point back to  

\[
\mathcal{F}= \bigl\{h\in[0,1]^N\;|\;\sum_i h_i = N/2\bigr\}.
\]

Projection onto a box followed by a projection onto a **single equality** can be performed in one pass using the well‑known “clipping‑with‑lambda” technique:

1. Find a scalar `λ` such that  

   \[
   \sum_i \operatorname{clip}(h_i+λ,0,1) = N/2 .
   \]

2. The clipped vector is the projected point.  

The function `λ → Σ clip(h_i+λ,0,1)` is monotone, so `λ` can be obtained by a short binary search (≈30 iterations gives `1e‑12` accuracy, far cheaper than any LP).

### 4.  Exploiting symmetry
The autocorrelation is symmetric: `a_{-s}=a_s`.  
Empirically optimal solutions are symmetric around `x=1`.  
We therefore **enforce symmetry** after each projection:

```
h = (h + h[::-1]) / 2
```

The symmetry operation preserves the box constraints and the total sum, so the subsequent projection step only has to correct the tiny numerical drift.

Enforcing symmetry halves the effective number of degrees of freedom, speeds up convergence and frequently yields a lower `C5`.
