**Overview**

We will treat the problem as a *continuous physics‑based simulation* followed by a *global deterministic refinement*.  
The simulation builds a feasible configuration by **simultaneously moving centres** and **inflating radii** under soft forces that mimic the hard constraints (wall containment and non‑overlap).  
When the system reaches a quasi‑steady state we freeze the geometry, compute the exact maximal radii for the fixed centres, and finally run a **small‑scale global nonlinear optimisation** (trust‑region) that tweaks the centres to remove any remaining slack.  

The whole pipeline differs fundamentally from the earlier “max‑radius → hill‑climb → SciPy” approach: here the radii are *grown continuously* while the centres are *pushed by forces*, rather than first fixing centres and then moving them. This tends to discover layouts where circles share walls and each other more symmetrically, often leading to higher total radii.

### 1. Physics‑based growth & repulsion simulation

#### 1.1 Variables
- `c_i = (x_i, y_i)` – centre of circle *i* (continuous, bounded in `[0,1]²`).
- `r_i` – radius (continuous, ≥ 0).

#### 1.2 Forces
For each circle we define three types of forces acting on its centre:

1. **Wall repulsion**  
   For each wall (left, right, bottom, top) compute the signed distance  
   \[
   d_{i}^{\text{L}} = x_i - r_i,\quad
   d_{i}^{\text{R}} = (1-x_i) - r_i,\quad
   d_{i}^{\text{B}} = y_i - r_i,\quad
   d_{i}^{\text{T}} = (1-y_i) - r_i .
   \]  
   If any `d` becomes negative (the circle would cross the wall) we apply a restoring force proportional to `-d` in the direction opposite to the wall, e.g.  
   \[
   \mathbf{F}_{i}^{\text{L}} = k_w \, \max(-d_{i}^{\text{L}},0) \, (-1,0),
   \]  
   and similarly for the other three walls. `k_w` is a wall‑stiffness constant (≈ 10).

2. **Pairwise repulsion**  
   For each unordered pair \((i,j)\) compute the centre distance  
   \(d_{ij}= \|c_i-c_j\|\) and the *gap*  
   \(g_{ij}= d_{ij} - (r_i+r_j)\).  
   If `g_ij` < 0 we push the circles apart with a force  
   \[
   \mathbf{F}_{ij}= k_p \, (-g_{ij}) \frac{c_i-c_j}{d_{ij}} ,
   \]  
   and add the opposite to circle j. `k_p` (≈ 5) controls how strongly overlaps are corrected.

3. **Damping**  
   To prevent oscillations we add a velocity‑dependent damping term `-γ v_i` with `γ≈1`.

The total force on circle *i* is the sum of its wall and pairwise contributions.

#### 1.3 Radius growth law
All radii increase *uniformly* at a small base rate `α` (≈ 1e‑3 per iteration).  
When a circle experiences a non‑zero repulsive force (i.e. it is touching a wall or another circle), its growth is *tempered*:
\[
\dot r_i = \alpha \; \prod_{k\in\{L,R,B,T\}} \mathbf{1}_{d_i^k>0}
          \times \prod_{j\neq i} \mathbf{1}_{g_{ij}>0},
\]
i.e. a circle only grows if it is **not** currently overlapping any constraint. This rule guarantees that radii never become illegal.

#### 1.4 Time integration
We use a simple explicit Euler scheme with timestep `Δt = 0.02`:

```
v_i ← v_i + Δt * (F_i - γ v_i)
c_i ← c_i + Δt * v_i
r_i ← r_i + Δt * dot_r_i
```

After each step we clip centers to `[0,1]` (to avoid numerical drift) and enforce `r_i ≥ 0`.

#### 1.5 Termination
The simulation stops when **no radius grows** for a full sweep (all `dot_r_i` are zero) **and** the maximum norm of all forces drops below a tiny threshold (`1e‑6`). At that point the configuration is *locally jammed*: every circle is either touching a wall or another circle, and no further inflation is possible without moving a centre.

### 2. Exact maximal radii for the frozen centres

When the simulation terminates we have a feasible set of centres `{c_i}` but the radii may be slightly undersized because the growth rule is conservative.  
We now compute the *exact* maximal radii that each centre can support while keeping all other radii fixed:

For each circle *i*,
\[
r_i^{\max}= \min\Bigl(
               x_i,\; 1-x_i,\; y_i,\; 1-y_i,\;
               \min_{j\neq i}\; \|c_i-c_j\| - r_j
           \Bigr).
\]
All `r_j` on the right‑hand side are the *current* radii (starting from the values produced by the simulation).  
We iterate this update over all circles **simultaneously** (vectorised) until the radii converge (change < 1e‑9). Because each update only reduces a radius, the process is monotone and terminates quickly (≤ 30 iterations). The final radii are the **largest possible** for the given centre positions.

### 3. Global refinement via constrained nonlinear optimisation

The centre positions obtained after the simulation are already good, but a few millimetres of slack often remain that can be exploited by a small coordinated motion of many circles. We therefore perform a final optimisation with the *exact* constraints:

#### 3.1 Decision vector
\[
\mathbf{v}= (x_1,y_1,\dots,x_{26},y_{26}) \in \mathbb{R}^{52}.
\]

#### 3.2 Objective
Maximise the sum of radii **as a function of the centres**:
\[
\max_{\mathbf{v}} \; S(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^{26} r_i(\mathbf{v}),
\]
where `r_i(v)` is the maximal radius for centre *i* given the other centres (the same formula as in §2).  
Although `r_i(v)` is not analytically smooth everywhere (it is the minimum of many affine/Euclidean terms), we can treat it as a *piecewise* function and let the optimizer evaluate it numerically.

#### 3.3 Constraints
- **Box constraints** for each coordinate: `0 ≤ x_i ≤ 1`, `0 ≤ y_i ≤ 1`.

No explicit inequality constraints are needed because the radii are *computed* from the centres; the objective itself automatically penalises infeasible arrangements (the radius will become negative, which we clamp to zero). However, to keep the optimizer inside the feasible region we also add **soft barrier terms**:
\[
B(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_i \bigl( \max(0, -x_i)^2 + \max(0, x_i-1)^2 + \max(0, -y_i)^2 + \max(0, y_i-1)^2 \bigr).
\]
We then minimise `-S(v) + λ B(v)` with a small λ (≈ 1e‑4). This keeps the iterates inside the square while allowing the optimizer to explore the interior.

#### 3.4 Optimiser choice
`scipy.optimize.minimize` with method **'L-BFGS-B'** works well for box‑constrained problems and accepts a smooth (or piecewise‑smooth) objective. Because the objective can be evaluated cheaply (O(N²) per call, N=26), we can afford several thousand iterations.

We supply the centres from §2 as the initial guess, set `maxiter=2000`, and use a tolerance of `1e‑9` on the gradient norm.

#### 3.5 Post‑processing
After the L‑BFGS‑B run we recompute the exact maximal radii (the same fixed‑centre routine from §2) to guarantee feasibility, and compute the final sum `S_final`.
