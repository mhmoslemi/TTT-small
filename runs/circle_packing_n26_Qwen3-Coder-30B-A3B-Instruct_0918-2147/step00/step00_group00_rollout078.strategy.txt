**Goal** – Find positions and radii for 26 circles inside the unit square that maximise the total sum of radii while obeying the non‑overlap and containment constraints, achieving a sum ≥ 2.636.

**Core Idea – Global Stochastic Optimisation with Adaptive Radius Inflation**

Instead of solving a deterministic convex programme or searching a fixed lattice, we treat the 78 real‑valued variables (26 × (x,y) + 26 × r) as a continuous optimisation problem and apply a *simulated‑annealing / evolutionary‑strategy* loop that explicitly **inflates** radii during the search. The algorithm alternates two complementary moves:

1. **Position‑only perturbations** – relocate circle centres while keeping radii fixed, to discover more free space.
2. **Uniform radius inflation** – increase all radii by the same tiny amount, then immediately shrink only the circles that violate constraints back to the maximal feasible size for the current layout (computed analytically).  

By repeatedly inflating and re‑adjusting we drive the system toward a configuration where many circles are simultaneously “tight” against walls or neighbours – exactly the situation that maximises the sum of radii. The stochastic element avoids getting trapped in symmetric local optima that hurt pure gradient‑based methods.

The strategy is divided into five phases:

| Phase | Purpose | Key Operations |
|------|----------|----------------|
| **A – Structured Initialisation** | Produce a feasible start that already distributes circles fairly evenly. | Generate a *hexagonal seed* (like previous methods) but assign **different initial radii** based on distance to the nearest wall (larger near centre, smaller near corners). Scale the whole pattern so that the smallest radius is 0.015, guaranteeing feasibility. |
| **B – Adaptive Inflation Loop** | Grow the total radius as much as possible while keeping feasibility. | Repeatedly: <br>• Determine the *global inflation step* Δ = α · mean(r) (α starts at 0.04 and decays). <br>• Add Δ to every radius: r′ = r + Δ. <br>• For each circle, compute its *max‑allowable radius* given the current centres and all other radii (see “Exact Local Feasibility” below). Replace r′ by the minimum of r′ and that bound. <br>• If the total sum increased by less than a tolerance ε (e.g., 1e‑6) for K consecutive iterations, proceed to Phase C. |

Iterate this sweep a few times (≤3) – the radii quickly converge to a locally maximal feasible set.

The output of Phase E is the final packing.

### Exact Local Feasibility (used in Phases B, C, E)

Given a fixed centre set **C** and a provisional radius vector **r**, the maximal feasible radius for circle *i* is the minimum of three quantities:

1. Distance to each of the four walls:
   - d_L = xᵢ
   - d_R = 1‑xᵢ
   - d_B = yᵢ
   - d_T = 1‑yᵢ

2. For every other circle *j*, the distance to its centre minus its current radius:
   - d_ij = ‖cᵢ‑cⱼ‖₂ – rⱼ

3. The current radius rᵢ (if we are shrinking).

Thus  
`r_i^max = min( d_L, d_R, d_B, d_T, min_j d_ij )`.  

This expression is cheap (O(N) per circle) and yields the exact feasible bound; we never need any linearisation or approximations.

### Implementation Blueprint (top‑level functions)

1. **`make_hex_seed(n, margin)`** – builds the initial hexagonal pattern, computes per‑circle initial radii based on wall distances, rescales to guarantee a minimum radius of 0.015. Returns `(centers, radii)`.

2. **`max_feasible_radius(i, centers, radii)`** – implements the “Exact Local Feasibility” formula; used throughout.

3. **`inflate_radii(centers, radii, alpha)`** – adds a global Δ = α·mean(r) to every radius, then clamps each radius with `max_feasible_radius`. Returns the updated radii and the increase in total sum.

4. **`anneal_centers(centers, radii, temp0, gamma, sigma0, steps)`** – runs the SA loop described in Phase C; modifies centres in‑place, updates radii opportunistically (when a move yields a larger feasible radius for the moved circle). Returns updated centres, radii, and the best sum observed during the block.

5. **`mixed_cycle(centers, radii, params)`** – orchestrates the alternating inflation‑SA cycles (Phases B↔C↔D) until convergence criteria are met.

6. **`polish_radii(centers, radii, beta, passes)`** – performs the exact local inflation and overlap‑resolution sweep of Phase E.

7. **`run_packing()`** – top‑level driver: generate seed → mixed_cycle → polish_radii → final validation (using the supplied `validate_packing`). Returns the final arrays and the sum.

All functions are pure (no closures, no lambdas), use only `numpy` for vectorised distance calculations and `random`/`numpy.random` for stochastic moves. No file I/O, network access, or external solvers are required, satisfying the execution constraints.

| Parameter | Reasonable Default | Adjustment Guidance |

| M (SA steps per block) | 200 | Balances between centre exploration and radius growth. |

These defaults have been selected from empirical experience on similar packing problems; modest adjustments can be made if the early runs fall short of the 2.636 target.

### Expected Failure Modes & Mitigations

1. **Numerical Overlap after Polishing** – The greedy inflation may produce a tiny overlap due to floating‑point round‑off. The overlap‑resolution sweep halves the overlapping amount, guaranteeing feasibility. Final validation will catch any residual issue; if needed, a final uniform shrink of `1e‑7` is applied.

2. **Stagnation in SA** – If the temperature decays too fast, the algorithm may stop moving centres before enough gaps open. The mixed‑cycle structure automatically restarts a new inflation block, raising the temperature back to `T₀` for the next SA block, thus re‑injecting mobility.

3. **Excessive Runtime** – The main cost is O(N²) distance calculations per inflation and per SA move. With N=26 this is negligible; even 10⁵ SA proposals finish in <0.5 s on a single CPU. The algorithm can safely be bounded by a hard iteration limit (e.g., total SA proposals ≤ 2·10⁶).

4. **Getting Trapped in Very Symmetric Layout** – The random displacement plus occasional global translation break symmetry quickly. The alternating inflation also perturbs the radii non‑uniformly, further discouraging symmetric dead‑ends.

5. **Initial Seed Too Sparse** – If the seed’s minimum radius is set too low, early inflations may be very slow. The chosen margin and minimum radius (≈0.015) guarantee a reasonably dense start; if experiments show very slow growth, increase the seed density (reduce margin).
