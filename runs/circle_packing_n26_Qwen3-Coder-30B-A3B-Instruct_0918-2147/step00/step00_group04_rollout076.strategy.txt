**Title – “Iterated‐Relax‑&‑Fit with a Global Geometric Decomposition”**

The algorithm treats the packing problem as a *sequence of easier sub‑problems* that are solved exactly (or to provable optimality) and then stitched together.  
Instead of optimizing all 26 circles simultaneously (as in the SLSQP or annealing approaches) we:

1. **Decompose the unit square into a small number of convex cells** whose shapes are simple (triangles, rectangles, or isosceles right‑triangles).  
2. **Within each cell we solve a tiny “max‑sum‑radii” sub‑problem** (1–4 circles) **exactly** by linear programming (LP) because the cell boundaries are linear and the circles can be assumed to touch either a cell edge or another circle.  
3. **Merge neighboring cells iteratively**: when two adjacent cells are combined we solve a new LP that contains all circles belonging to the two cells, but we keep the *relative* positions of circles that already touch each other fixed. The only free variables are the radii of circles that lie on the shared interface; the LP decides how to distribute the remaining space between them.  
4. **After the whole square has been merged** we obtain a feasible configuration of 26 circles whose sum of radii is *locally optimal* with respect to every possible refinement that respects the final adjacency graph.  
5. **Global polishing** – a short gradient‑based “force‑directed” refinement (with logarithmic barrier terms) is applied to the whole system to eliminate any remaining slack and to push circles that are not touching anything outward, increasing the total sum. Because the LP‑stage already produced a configuration where most circles are already in contact with a neighbour or a wall, only a few iterations are needed.

The whole pipeline is deterministic, needs no stochastic restarts, and each intermediate optimisation problem is convex (LP or SOCP) and therefore solved to global optimality.  
Below is the step‑by‑step plan, the mathematical formulation of each stage, implementation notes, and a discussion of likely failure modes.

---

### 1. Geometric decomposition of the square

* **Goal** – create a hierarchy of cells such that the total number of circles that can be placed inside each leaf cell is known a‑priori (by a simple bound) and small (≤ 4).  
* **Method** – start from the four corners and the central region:
  * Partition the square into **nine** rectangles:
    * Four corner rectangles of size `a × a`.
    * Four edge rectangles of size `a × (1‑2a)`.
    * One central rectangle of size `(1‑2a) × (1‑2a)`.
  * Choose `a = 0.25`. This yields 9 cells each containing at most a handful of circles when we later allocate them.
* **Why this shape** – each cell has at most two orthogonal edges of the outer square, so circles placed near a corner can grow large while still leaving room for neighbours in adjacent cells.

---

### 2. Initial allocation of circles to cells

* **Heuristic** – we wish to distribute 26 circles roughly proportional to cell area, but also give more to corners because they allow larger radii.
* **Allocation table (example)**  

| Cell | Area | Desired #circles |
|------|------|------------------|
| 4 corners            | 0.0625 each | 5 each (total 20) |
| 4 edges (mid‑sides)  | 0.1875 each | 1 each (total 4) |
| centre               | 0.5625      | 2 (total 2) |
| **Sum**              | 1          | **26** |

Thus corners receive 5 circles each, edges 1 each, centre 2.  
If the solver later cannot accommodate the exact count we will shift a circle from a low‑density cell to a neighbour (see §5).

* **Placement of circle *centers* inside a leaf cell** – we *do not* choose them yet. Instead we treat them as *variables* to be solved in the LP of that leaf.

---

### 3. Exact LP formulation for a leaf cell

Consider a leaf cell `C` that is a convex polygon (here a rectangle).  
Let `k` be the number of circles assigned to `C`. For each circle `i = 1…k` we introduce variables:

* `x_i, y_i` – coordinates of the centre (restricted to the interior of `C`).
* `r_i` – radius (≥ 0).

**Constraints**

1. **Boundary to the cell** – for each edge `e` of `C` with outward normal `n_e` and distance function `d_e(p) ≥ 0` for points inside `C`:
   ```
   d_e( (x_i, y_i) ) ≥ r_i .
   ```
   Because `C` is a rectangle, each constraint is linear:
   * `x_i - r_i ≥ x_min`
   * `x_i + r_i ≤ x_max`
   * `y_i - r_i ≥ y_min`
   * `y_i + r_i ≤ y_max`.

2. **Non‑overlap inside the cell** – for every pair `i < j`:
   ```
   (x_i - x_j)^2 + (y_i - y_j)^2 ≥ (r_i + r_j)^2 .
   ```
   This is quadratic, but for a *fixed small k (≤ 4)* we can linearise it by **introducing an auxiliary variable** `d_ij` that upper‑bounds the Euclidean distance:
   ```
   d_ij ≥ sqrt( (x_i - x_j)^2 + (y_i - y_j)^2 )
   d_ij ≥ r_i + r_j .
   ```
   The first inequality is a **second‑order cone (SOC)** constraint; the second is linear. Hence the whole leaf problem is an **LP+SOC** (an SOCP) that CVXPY solves to global optimum quickly.

3. **Optional “tightness” objective** – we maximise the **sum of radii** *within this cell*:
   ```
   maximize   Σ_i r_i .
   ```

Because the cell is isolated, the optimal solution will push circles to touch either the cell walls or each other, giving a *locally optimal* distribution for that cell.

**Output** – the optimal radii `r_i`, centre coordinates `c_i`, and also the *active set*: which constraints are tight (distance equals `r_i+r_j` or a wall constraint equals `r_i`). These active contacts are stored for later merging.

---

### 4. Hierarchical merging of adjacent cells

After solving all leaf cells we possess a set of circles grouped into 9 clusters, each with a known adjacency graph (which circles touch each other).  

We now **merge cells pair‑wise** following a binary tree over the 9 cells. At each merge step we solve another SOCP that includes:

* **All circles from the two child cells** (their centres and radii are *variables* again).  
* **All constraints that were already active inside each child** are **kept as equalities** (i.e., we freeze the relative positions of circles that were already touching). This drastically reduces the dimensionality of the merge problem; only the *degrees of freedom* are the translations/rotations of the whole child cluster and the radii of circles that lie on the shared interface.

* **Interface constraints** – for every pair of circles `(i from left child, j from right child)` that lie near the common border we add a non‑overlap SOC constraint:
  ```
  ‖c_i - c_j‖_2 ≥ r_i + r_j .
  ```
  Usually only circles whose centres are within a distance ≈ 0.2 of the border can become active, so we filter pairs to keep the problem small.

* **Boundary constraints** – circles that are adjacent to the outer square retain the original wall constraints (as linear inequalities).

* **Objective** – maximise the sum of radii of *all* circles in the merged cluster.

Because the internal geometry of each child is already tight, the optimizer will mainly *translate* each child as a rigid body (or a slight shear if needed) to allow a better fit across the interface, and it may slightly increase radii of interface circles (the LP will allocate extra slack to them). The problem size stays modest (≤ 12 circles per merge) and solves in milliseconds.

We repeat this merging until a **single global cluster** containing all 26 circles is obtained. The final SOCP therefore gives a *globally feasible* configuration where every circle either touches another circle or a wall, and no circle can be enlarged without violating a constraint (local optimality with respect to the fixed adjacency structure).

---

### 5. Adaptive re‑allocation / “circle migration”

During merging we may discover that a child cluster cannot accommodate its prescribed number of circles without severe overlap (the SOCP becomes infeasible). In that case we **migrate a circle** from the overloaded cluster to a neighbour:

* Identify the *least‑constrained* circle in the problematic cluster (the one with smallest dual variable on its wall constraints).  
* Remove it from that cluster and add it to the adjacent cluster’s circle count.  
* Re‑solve the two child SOCPs separately, then continue merging.

Because the total number of circles is modest, this migration step will be invoked at most a few times and resolves incompatibilities that stem from the initial heuristic allocation.

---

### 6. Global polishing via barrier‑based gradient flow

After the hierarchical merge we possess a feasible configuration where many circles are already touching each other or a wall, but some circles may still have *slack* (no contact). To increase the sum of radii we run a *few* iterations of a continuous barrier‑based optimisation:

* Variables: all `x_i, y_i, r_i`.  
* Objective: maximise `Σ r_i`.  
* Constraints encoded as **log‑barrier penalties** added to the objective:
  ```
  Φ = - Σ r_i
      - μ * Σ_i [ log( x_i - r_i ) + log(1 - x_i - r_i )
                + log( y_i - r_i ) + log(1 - y_i - r_i ) ]
      - μ * Σ_{i<j} log( ‖c_i - c_j‖_2 - r_i - r_j )
  ```
  with a tiny barrier weight `μ = 1e‑4`.  
* Perform *projected gradient ascent*: compute the gradient of `Φ`, take a step size determined by a simple backtracking line search, and then **project** the new variables back onto the feasible set (clipping to `[0,1]`, enforcing `r_i ≥ 0`).  

Because the starting point is already close to the feasible boundary, only *1–3* iterations are enough for the circles to slide into any remaining gaps, increasing the total sum by ~1–2 %. The barrier ensures we never cross a constraint, so the final configuration remains valid.

---

### 7. Final validation and tiny post‑processing

* Re‑solve a **pure SOCP** (same formulation as in §3 but with all circles together) using **high‑precision solver settings** (`abstol=1e‑12`, `reltol=1e‑12`). This guarantees that any rounding error introduced by the gradient steps is eliminated.
* If any constraint is violated by more than `1e‑13`, reduce the offending radius by the excess plus a safety margin `1e‑13`. This adjustment changes the total sum by an utterly negligible amount and ensures the supplied `validate_packing` routine will accept the solution.

The routine returns:
* `centers` – an `(26,2)` NumPy array of the final coordinates.
* `radii` – a length‑26 array.
* `sum_radii` – the scalar sum (float).

### 9. Anticipated failure modes & mitigations

| Failure mode | Why it can happen | Countermeasure |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Leaf SOCP infeasibility** (too many circles in a tiny rectangle) | The heuristic allocation may over‑populate a corner cell. | Detect infeasibility, automatically drop one circle and re‑allocate to the neighbour; repeat until feasible. |
| **Merging creates an infeasible cluster** | Rigid‑body translation may not free enough space for the interface circles. | Use the *migration* step to move a circle across the border before re‑merging. |
| **Barrier gradient diverges** | Too large step size or too high barrier weight. | Use a conservative back‑tracking line search; start with `μ = 1e‑5` and increase only if no improvement is seen. |
| **Numerical precision loss in final SOCP** | Accumulated round‑off from many solves. | Invoke the final high‑precision solve with very tight tolerances; the problem size (26 variables) is trivial for ECOS/OSQP. |
| **Total sum < target** | Over‑conservative allocation (e.g., giving too many circles to corners, leaving edges under‑populated). | After an initial run, compute the sum; if below the target, redo the allocation with a slight reshuffle (move one circle from a corner to an edge) and repeat the pipeline. ### 10. Why this strategy is materially different

* **Problem decomposition** – Instead of tackling a 78‑dimensional non‑linear problem at once, we split the domain into tiny convex cells, solve exact sub‑problems, and only later merge them.  
* **Rigid‑body preservation of internal contacts** – During merging we keep already‑tight contacts fixed, turning the merge into a low‑dimensional adjustment rather than a full re‑optimisation.  
* **Explicit migration mechanism** – The algorithm can *move circles* between cells when a local allocation proves impossible, a capability absent in the pure SLSQP or plain annealing schemes.  
* **Hybrid exact + gradient polishing** – The bulk of the work is done by provably optimal SOCPs; the only non‑convex step is a brief barrier‑based gradient ascent, far less prone to getting trapped than a full‑scale non‑linear optimizer.  
* **Deterministic hierarchy** – No random restarts or simulated‑annealing schedules are required for correctness; randomness is only optional for seeding, making the method robust and reproducible.

This decomposition‑first, exact‑solve‑later philosophy yields a clean, mathematically grounded pipeline that can be implemented with only `numpy` and `cvxpy`, respects all constraints, and is expected to surpass the target sum of radii reliably.
