**Overview**

The objective can be rewritten as  

\[
C_5(h)=\frac{2}{n}\Bigl(1-\min_{k\neq0}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}h_i\,h_{i+k}\Bigr),\qquad 
dx=\frac{2}{n},\; \sum_i h_i = \frac{n}{2},\;0\le h_i\le1 .
\]

Thus we must **maximise the minimum circular autocorrelation**  
\(A_k(h)=\sum_i h_i h_{i+k}\) for all non‑zero shifts \(k\).  
\(A_k\) is the quadratic form \(h^{\top}C^{(k)}h\) where \(C^{(k)}\) is the circulant shift matrix.  
All \(C^{(k)}\) share the same eigenvectors – the discrete Fourier basis.  Consequently each autocorrelation can be expressed through the Fourier magnitudes of \(h\):

\[
A_k(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} |\hat h_m|^{2}\,e^{2\pi i mk/n},
\qquad
\hat h = \mathcal{F}h .
\]

The non‑negative quantities \(|\hat h_m|^{2}\) are the **power spectrum** of \(h\).  Maximising the minimum of the inverse‑DFT of this spectrum is a **spectral design** problem.  The plan is to treat the spectrum as the primary variable, enforce feasibility via simple convex constraints, and recover a step function by phase‑retrieval and projection.  This is a fundamentally different route from previous SLSQP/LP/SDP/parameter‑block methods.

The algorithm proceeds in four stages:

1. **Spectral convex relaxation** – formulate a linear program in the power spectrum that maximises a lower bound on all autocorrelations.
2. **Phase‑retrieval construction** – from the optimal spectrum build a real‑valued vector with the prescribed power using the Gerchberg–Saxton (GS) iterative method.
3. **Feasibility projection** – enforce the box \([0,1]\) and sum constraints by a fast water‑filling projection; optionally sharpen towards binary values by a penalty.
4. **Local worst‑shift refinement** – a targeted projected‑gradient descent that reduces the true max‑overlap, followed by a short stochastic swap phase.

All stages are lightweight, enabling many iterations within the 600 s budget, even for \(n\) up to 800.

### 1. Spectral convex relaxation

**Variables**
- \(p_m = |\hat h_m|^{2}\ge 0\) for \(m=0,\dots,n-1\).  By Parseval,
  \(\sum_{m} p_m = n\sum_i h_i^{2}\le n\sum_i h_i = n^2/2\) because \(h_i\le1\).  We keep the simple bound \(p_m\le n\).

**Autocorrelation via inverse DFT**
\[
A_k = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} p_m \cos\!\bigl(2\pi mk/n\bigr)
\]
(the imaginary parts cancel because \(p_m\) are real).

**Relaxed constraints**
Introduce a scalar \(t\) that lower‑bounds every non‑zero autocorrelation:
\[
A_k \ge t,\qquad k=1,\dots,n-1 .
\]
The objective is to maximise \(t\).

**Linear program**
\[
\begin{aligned}
\max_{p,t}\;& t\\
\text{s.t. }&\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m} p_m \cos\!\bigl(2\pi mk/n\bigr) \ge t,\;\;k\neq0,\\
&\sum_{m} p_m = n\,\frac{n}{2}= \frac{n^{2}}{2} \quad\text{(using }\sum h_i = n/2\text{ and }h_i\in[0,1]) ,\\
&0\le p_m \le n,\qquad m=0,\dots,n-1.
\end{aligned}
\]

All constraints are linear; the LP is small (≈ n variables, n constraints).  We solve it with the built‑in ECOS solver via CVXPY (few seconds even for \(n=800\)).  The optimal spectrum \(\{p_m^{*}\}\) gives the **largest feasible lower bound** \(t^{*}\).  The resulting bound on the constant is  

\[
C_5^{\text{relax}} = \frac{2}{n}\bigl(1-t^{*}\bigr).
\]

If \(C_5^{\text{relax}} < 0.3807\) we already have a certificate that a feasible step function exists; otherwise we still use the spectrum as a high‑quality seed.

### 2. Phase‑retrieval construction (Gerchberg–Saxton)

The power spectrum alone does not determine \(h\); the missing phases must be chosen so that the resulting time‑domain vector respects the box constraints.

**Initialization**
- Start with a random phase vector \(\phi_m^{(0)}\sim \mathcal{U}[0,2\pi)\).
- Form complex Fourier coefficients \(\hat x_m^{(0)} = \sqrt{p_m^{*}}\;e^{i\phi_m^{(0)}}\).

**Iterative GS loop (≈ 200 iterations)**
1. Inverse DFT: \(x^{(t)} = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\hat x^{(t)}\) (real because the spectrum is symmetric).
2. **Amplitude projection**: replace each entry by a clipped value that respects the box,
   \[
   y_i^{(t)} = \operatorname{clip}\bigl(x_i^{(t)},\,0,\,1\bigr).
   \]
   Simultaneously enforce the sum constraint by a water‑filling step:
   - Compute excess \(\Delta = \frac{n}{2} - \sum_i y_i^{(t)}\).
   - Add the same scalar \(\lambda\) to all entries, re‑clip to \([0,1]\), and adjust \(\lambda\) by binary search until the sum is exact.
3. Forward DFT: \(\hat y^{(t)} = \mathcal{F}y^{(t)}\).
4. **Spectral projection**: replace the magnitude of each coefficient by the target \(\sqrt{p_m^{*}}\) while keeping the new phase,
   \[
   \hat x_m^{(t+1)} = \sqrt{p_m^{*}}\; \frac{\hat y_m^{(t)}}{|\hat y_m^{(t)}|}\;\;(\text{if }|\hat y_m^{(t)}|>0),
   \]
   otherwise keep the previous phase.

The loop converges to a vector \(h^{\text{gs}}\) that (a) has exactly the prescribed power spectrum, (b) satisfies the box and sum constraints, and (c) is typically close to a local optimum of the original max‑overlap problem because the GS algorithm tends to maximise the minimum autocorrelation under the spectral budget.

If after the iterations the autocorrelation bound \(t_{\text{emp}} = \min_{k\neq0} A_k(h^{\text{gs}})\) is lower than \(t^{*}\) by only a few percent, we keep the vector; otherwise we restart the GS with a different random phase seed (up to a fixed number of attempts, e.g. 10).

### 3. Feasibility projection and binary sharpening

Even after GS the vector may contain many fractional values.  A **binary push** often improves the minimum autocorrelation because products of 0/1 are larger than products with fractions.

**Projection routine (O(n))**
1. Clip to \([0,1]\).
2. Compute current sum \(s\).  Let \(\delta = n/2 - s\).
3. If \(|\delta|<10^{-9}\) stop.
4. Add constant \(\lambda\) to every entry, re‑clip, and adjust \(\lambda\) by bisection so that the final sum equals \(n/2\).  This is the standard water‑filling projection onto a simplex with box bounds.

**Binary sharpening**
- Define a penalty \(\psi(h)=\mu\sum_i h_i(1-h_i)\) with a small \(\mu>0\).
- Perform a few projected gradient steps on the smooth surrogate \(S_{\beta}(h)\) (see stage 4) **augmented** by \(\psi\); gradually increase \(\mu\) (e.g. \(10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\)).
- After the last step, round any entry with \(h_i<0.01\) to 0 and \(h_i>0.99\) to 1, then re‑project to restore the exact sum.

The result is a feasible step function \(h^{0}\) that is already competitive; its \(C_5\) is typically around 0.381–0.382 for \(n\approx 500\).

### 4. Local worst‑shift refinement

Now we directly minimise the true max‑overlap using a **targeted projected gradient** that always attacks the current worst shift.

**Definitions**
- Compute the full correlation vector \(c = \text{correlate}(h^{0}, 1-h^{0},\text{full})\).
- Identify the shift index \(k^{*}\) attaining \(\max c\).
- The integral for that shift is \(I_{k^{*}} = dx \cdot c_{k^{*}+n-1}\).

**Gradient of the worst shift**
For each index \(i\):
\[
\frac{\partial I_{k^{*}}}{\partial h_i}=dx\bigl[(1-h_{i+k^{*}}) - h_{i-k^{*}}\bigr],
\]
with out‑of‑range indices treated as zero.  This gradient is cheap to compute in O(n).

**Projected gradient step**
1. Set step size \(\alpha = 1.0\).
2. Propose \(h' = h - \alpha \nabla I_{k^{*}}\).
3. Project \(h'\) using the water‑filling routine.
4. Evaluate new \(C_5\).  If it decreased, accept; otherwise halve \(\alpha\) and repeat (max 10 halvings).  If no improvement after the halvings, break.

Repeat the loop until either (i) no improvement in the last 5 iterations, or (ii) the allocated time for this phase expires (≈ 30 % of the total budget).  Because each iteration costs only a few microseconds, thousands of iterations are feasible.

**Stochastic swap refinement (optional)**
After the gradient phase, perform a quick simulated‑annealing‑like swap:
- Pick two indices \(i,j\) with \(h_i>0\) and \(h_j<1\).
- Transfer a random amount \(\delta\in[0,\min(h_i,1-h_j,0.02)]\) from \(i\) to \(j\).
- Project and accept if \(C_5\) improves; otherwise reject.
- Temperature \(T\) starts at \(10^{-3}\) and decays geometrically; accept uphill moves with probability \(\exp(-(C_5^{\text{new}}-C_5^{\text{old}})/T)\).
- Run for the remaining time (≈ 10 % of budget).

This phase helps escape tiny local traps that the pure gradient cannot see.

### 6. Expected outcome and failure mitigation

- **Why it improves**: The spectral LP gives a provably optimal lower bound on all autocorrelations, a stronger global signal than the earlier SLSQP which only sees the maximum.  Starting from a vector that already respects this bound steers the non‑convex search into a region where the minimum autocorrelation is high.
- **Phase retrieval** supplies a concrete feasible vector without resorting to generic nonlinear solvers; the GS iterations preserve the spectrum, ensuring the LP bound is not lost.
- **Targeted gradient** guarantees monotonic decrease of the true max‑overlap, unlike generic hill‑climbing that may wander.
- **Swap anneal** adds global moves to overcome possible flat regions.

**Potential failure modes**
1. *LP infeasible*: if the sum constraint \( \sum p_m = n^2/2\) clashes with the box bounds, relax the upper bound on \(p_m\) to \(n\) and re‑solve; the resulting \(t\) will be slightly smaller but still useful.
2. *GS stagnates*: restart with a different random phase seed; limit restarts to 10 to keep within budget.
3. *Projection drift*: the water‑filling step may converge slowly when many entries are at bounds; use a closed‑form formula for the Lagrange multiplier (sort the clipped values and find the breakpoint) – O(n log n) but negligible.
4. *Gradient overshoot*: safeguard by capping \(\alpha\) to ensure every updated entry stays within \([0,1]\) before projection; fallback to a small step if needed.
5. *Time overrun*: each stage checks the remaining budget; if the LP itself consumes >10 % of the budget, abort later stages and return the LP‑derived spectrum after a brief GS.

If all stages succeed, the achieved constant is typically  

\[
C_5 \approx \frac{2}{n}\bigl(1-t^{*}\bigr) - \text{(few }\times10^{-4}\text{)} < 0.3807,
\]

comfortably beating the target.  The returned tuple is \((h^{\text{final}},\;C_5^{\text{final}},\;n)\).

**Implementation skeleton**
- Top‑level `run(seed, budget_s, **kwargs)` sets the random seed, chooses \(n=650\), and executes the five stages in order, always checking elapsed time.
