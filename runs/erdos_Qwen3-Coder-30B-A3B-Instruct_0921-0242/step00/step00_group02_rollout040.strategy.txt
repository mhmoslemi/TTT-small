### 1. Algebraic reformulation

Let \(h\in\mathbb R^n\) and define the symmetric matrix \(X=hh^{\!\top}\).  
For each shift \(k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\) introduce the linear functional  

\[
\Phi_k(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-k}X_{i,i+k}=R_k(h).
\]

The constraints on \(h\) become linear in the pair \((h,X)\):

* **Box**: \(0\le h_i\le 1\).  
* **Mass**: \(\mathbf 1^{\!\top}h = n/2\) (because \(\int h=1\) and \(dx=2/n\)).  
* **PSD linking**: \(X\succeq 0\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(X)=h\).  
  (The rank‑1 condition \(X=hh^{\!\top}\) is *non‑convex* and will be dropped.)

With a scalar variable \(s\) representing the hoped‑for worst correlation, the exact non‑convex problem is

\[
\max_{h,X,s}\; s\qquad
\text{s.t.}\;
\Phi_k(X)\ge s\;\forall k,\;
X=hh^{\!\top},\;
0\le h\le1,\;
\mathbf1^{\!\top}h=n/2 .
\]

Dropping the rank‑1 equality yields the **SDP relaxation**:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\max_{h,X,s}\;& s\\
\text{s.t. }& \Phi_k(X)\ge s\quad(k=1,\dots,n-1),\\
            & X\succeq0,\\
            & X_{ii}=h_i,\; i=0,\dots,n-1,\\
            & 0\le h_i\le1,\\
            & \mathbf1^{\!\top}h=n/2 .
\end{aligned}
\tag{R}
\]

Any feasible \((h,X,s)\) of (R) gives a *certificate* that the true optimum of the original problem is at most \(1-dx\,s\).  Moreover the optimal \(h^\star\) of (R) is a *fractional* vector that will serve as a high‑quality seed for rounding.

### 3. Random hyperplane rounding

From a PSD matrix we obtain a factorisation \(X^\star = V V^{\!\top}\) with \(V\in\mathbb R^{n\times r}\) (\(r\) = rank).  A standard **Gaussian‑hyperplane rounding** proceeds:

1. Draw a random Gaussian vector \(g\sim\mathcal N(0,I_r)\).  
2. Compute raw scores \(z = V g\in\mathbb R^n\).  By symmetry, \(\mathbb E[\operatorname{sign}(z_i)\operatorname{sign}(z_j)]=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(X^\star_{ij})\), preserving correlations on average.  
3. Convert the signs to a binary vector \(b\in\{0,1\}^n\) via a *threshold* \(\tau\):  
   \[
   b_i = \begin{cases}
          1 & \text{if }z_i\ge\tau,\\
          0 & \text{otherwise.}
        \end{cases}
   \]
   The threshold \(\tau\) is chosen so that the mass constraint is satisfied exactly:
   \[
   \tau = \operatorname{quantile}\bigl(z,\,1-\tfrac{n/2}{n}\bigr).
   \]
   Equivalently, we set the top \(n/2\) entries of \(z\) to 1 and the rest to 0.

The result \(b\) fulfills all hard constraints (binary, box, sum) and inherits, in expectation, the high autocorrelations encoded in \(X^\star\).

Because a single draw may be unlucky, we **sample** the rounding repeatedly (e.g. 200–500 times) and keep the best‑scoring \(b\) according to the true objective
\[
C_5(b)=1-dx\,\min_k R_k(b).
\]

Sampling is cheap: each draw only needs a matrix‑vector product \(Vg\) (cost \(O(nr)\)) and a sort for thresholding.

### 5. Fast local polishing (swap heuristic)

The binary vector \(b\) may still be improvable by tiny local moves that keep the mass exact.  A simple **pair‑swap** refinement works:

* Compute the full correlation vector \(R_k(b)=\sum_i b_i b_{i+k}\).  
* Identify the critical shift \(k^\star=\arg\min_k R_k(b)\).  
* Examine a limited set of candidate swaps \((i,j)\) with \(b_i=1,\; b_j=0\).  
  For a given pair the change in \(R_{k^\star}\) can be evaluated in constant time by counting how the pair participates in the sum for shift \(k^\star\).  
* Perform the swap that yields the largest increase in \(R_{k^\star}\) (i.e. the largest decrease in \(C_5\)).  
* Update the correlation vector incrementally (add/subtract the contributions of the two indices).  
* Repeat until no improving swap exists or a tiny iteration budget (e.g. 500 swaps) is exhausted.

Because only the worst shift is targeted, this greedy method converges quickly and never harms the objective.
