This is LINEAR in t! Great, so in the no-cross-terms case, each r(d) changes linearly with t.

The new r(d) = old_r(d) + (t - h[i]) * A_d

And we want to minimize max_d [old_r(d) + (t-h[i])*A_d] over t in the feasible range.

This is the minimax of linear functions, which is a 1D linear programming problem! The optimal t is at one of the intersection points of the lines, or at the boundary of the feasible range.

In the cross-term case (e.g., i+d = j), the change involves t²:
t*(1-s+t) - h[i]*(1-h[j]) = t - s*t + t² - h[i] + h[i]*h[j] = t² - (s-1)*t + (h[i]*h[j] - h[i])
So it's quadratic in t.

But the cross terms only occur for specific d values (d = j-i or d = i-j), so at most 2 lags have quadratic changes.

For the optimization:
- If no cross terms: minimize max of linear functions → optimal t is at an intersection point or boundary
- If cross terms: minimize max of (mostly linear + 2 quadratic) functions → can be solved by sampling or by finding intersection points

For simplicity, let me use a sampling approach: evaluate at maybe 50 points in the feasible range and pick the best. Since the function is the max of at most 2n-1 linear (or quadratic) functions, the max is a piecewise-linear (or piecewise-quadratic) convex function of t, so the minimum is well-defined.

Actually, for linear functions: max of lines is convex, and its minimum over an interval is at one of the "break points" (intersections) or at the endpoints. The number of breakpoints is at most 2n-2 (for 2n-1 lines). So I could find all breakpoints and check each one, but that's O(n²) work.

For practical purposes, sampling 100 points should be sufficient to get close to optimal.
