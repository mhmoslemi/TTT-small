### 1. Mathematical reformulation  

For a discretised step function  
\(h = (h_0,\dots ,h_{N-1})\) with \(0\le h_i\le 1\) and \(\sum_i h_i = N/2\)  

the objective used by the grader is  

\[
C_5(h)=\max_{k\in\{-N+1,\dots ,N-1\}}  \; \Delta_k(h),\qquad
\Delta_k(h)=dx\sum_{i}\,h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+k}\bigr) .
\]

(Indices that fall outside \([0,N-1]\) are simply omitted; this is exactly what
`np.correlate` does.)  

Since \(\sum_i h_i\) is constant,  

\[
\Delta_k(h)=\underbrace{\!\!dx\!\sum_{i}h_i}_{=1}
          \;-\;dx\sum_{i}h_i\,h_{i+k}
          = 1- A_k(h) ,
\]
where  

\[
A_k(h)=dx\sum_{i}h_i\,h_{i+k}
\]

is the (non‑periodic) autocorrelation of \(h\).  
Thus **minimising** the maximal overlap is equivalent to **maximising the
minimal autocorrelation** :

\[
\max_h\;\min_k A_k(h)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\min_h\;C_5(h)=1-\max_h\!\min_k A_k(h) .
\]

Consequently the algorithm can work directly on the quantity
\(A_k\) (the “worst” shift is the one with the **smallest** \(A_k\)).  

---

### 2. Core optimisation primitive – projected sub‑gradient ascent  

For a fixed shift \(s\) the autocorrelation is  

\[
A_s(h)=dx\sum_{i=0}^{N-1-s} h_i\,h_{i+s}.
\]

Its gradient w.r.t. the vector \(h\) (ignoring the constant factor \(dx\))
is

\[
\frac{\partial A_s}{\partial h_i}= 
\begin{cases}
h_{i+s}          & 0\le i\le N-1-s,\\[4pt]
0                & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\qquad
\frac{\partial A_s}{\partial h_{i+s}}=
\begin{cases}
h_i              & 0\le i\le N-1-s,\\[4pt]
0                & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

All other components have zero derivative.  
Hence for the *worst* shift  

\[
s^\*=\arg\min_s A_s(h)
\]

the ascent direction is a sparse vector that adds to each
pair \((i,i+s^\*)\) the product of the opposite entry.  This direction nudges
large entries toward the positions that are paired with them in the
worst shift, thereby increasing the smallest autocorrelation.

**Projected update**

1. **Gradient step**  
   \(h^{\text{tmp}} = h + \alpha\,g(s^\*)\)  with a step size
   \(\alpha = \alpha_0/\sqrt{t+1}\) ( \(\alpha_0\) tuned experimentally, e.g.
   0.2).  

2. **Projection onto the feasible set** – the *capped simplex*:  

   \[
   \mathcal{S}=\Bigl\{x\in[0,1]^N\;:\;\sum_i x_i = N/2\Bigr\}.
   \]

   The projection is done in two stages:
   * subtract a common offset \(\lambda\) so that the sum becomes \(N/2\);
   * clip the result to \([0,1]\);
   * if clipping changes the sum, recompute a new \(\lambda\) on the
     still‑free coordinates.
   An \(O(N\log N)\) sorting based algorithm (the “water‑filling”
   method for a bounded simplex) works robustly for all \(N\le 500\).

3. **Acceptance** – keep the new point if its
   \(C_5\) (computed by a lightweight correlation routine) is not larger
   than the current best; otherwise accept with a small simulated‑annealing
   probability to avoid being trapped in a local plateau.

Repeatedly applying this *worst‑shift ascent* rapidly improves the
minimal autocorrelation and therefore reduces the maximal overlap.

### 3. Complementary moves for diversity  

While the ascent direction based on the worst shift is very effective,
pure gradient steps can become stuck when many coordinates hit a bound.
To maintain diversity two extra move families are interleaved

* **Balanced transfer move** – pick a random index \(i\) and its partner
  \(j=i+s^\*\) (modulo the overlapping range); move a small amount
  \(\delta\) from the larger of \((h_i,h_j)\) to the smaller one, respecting
  \([0,1]\).  This directly reduces the term \(h_i(1-h_j)\) that appears in
  the worst‑shift overlap.

* **Random perturbation** – add a Gaussian bump of tiny variance
  (\(\sigma\approx0.01\)) to a random subset (≈10 % of entries) and
  re‑project.  Used periodically (every few hundred iterations) to
  re‑explore the neighbourhood.

These moves are cheap (linear in \(N\)) and keep the algorithm from
converging prematurely.

The total budget (≈ 600 s) is split into three phases.

| Phase | Fraction of budget | Activity |
|------|-------------------|----------|

Because each phase uses the same projection routine, the feasibility of the

### 6. Fast evaluation of the objective  

The heavy part of the loop is the computation of all \(\Delta_k\) values.
Instead of the naïve \(O(N^2)\) `np.correlate`, a fast FFT‑based convolution
is used:

1. Pad both \(h\) and \(1-h\) to length \(2N\) (zero‑padding).  
2. Compute the FFT of the padded arrays.  
3. Multiply the first FFT by the complex conjugate of the second (this yields
   the correlation).  
4. Inverse FFT, keep the real part, and multiply by \(dx\).

FFT of length \(2N\) costs \(O(N\log N)\); for \(N=200\) the runtime is
sub‑microsecond, leaving the budget for millions of evaluations.

The worst‑shift index and the autocorrelation vector are extracted from the
FFT result with a single `argmin`/`argmax` operation.

### 7. Failure‑mode handling  

| Possible problem | Mitigation |
|------------------|------------|
| **All entries stuck at 0/1** – gradient becomes zero. | Random perturbation moves (§3) are injected every 500 iterations. |
| **Projection stalls (mass trapped at bounds)** | Adaptive step‑size: if the norm of the step is larger than the free slack (sum of distances to bounds), reduce \(\alpha\) until the projected update changes the vector. |
| **DE slows down (many infeasible candidates)** | DE’s trial vectors are immediately projected; if a candidate after projection has an identical fitness to its parent, the mutation factor is temporarily increased. |
| **Time overrun** | Each phase checks the remaining wall‑clock time before starting a new
  iteration; the outer loop breaks as soon as the budget is exhausted, guaranteeing that `run` always returns a result. |
| **Numerical noise in FFT (tiny imaginary parts)** | Take only the real part and enforce non‑negativity of the correlation values by `np.clip(...,0, None)` before evaluating the max. |
