Let me re-examine my proof. I claimed that for non-negative sequences, $\max(a*a) \geq \|a\|_2^2 / \text{something}$. Let me check:

$(a*a)_{n-1} = \sum_i a_i a_{n-1-i}$

This is the inner product of $a$ and $\text{rev}(a)$. By Cauchy-Schwarz:
$(a*a)_{n-1} \leq \|a\|_2 \cdot \|\text{rev}(a)\|_2 = \|a\|_2^2$

So $(a*a)_{n-1} \leq \|a\|_2^2$, not $\geq$! The inequality goes the other way for the center lag.

And $\|a\|_2^2 = \sum a_i^2$. The maximum of the convolution is $\geq (a*a)_{n-1}$ (since it's the max over all lags), but it could be at a different lag.

Actually, for the all-ones sequence: $(a*a)_{n-1} = n = \|a\|_2^2$. And it's the max.

For a general non-negative sequence, $(a*a)_{n-1} = \sum a_i a_{n-1-i} \leq \sum a_i^2 = \|a\|_2^2$ (by Cauchy-Schwarz, with equality iff $a$ is palindromic).

So for non-palindromic sequences, the center lag is NOT the max, and the max could be at a different lag.

The maximum of the full convolution: $\max_k (a*a)_k$. We know $\sum_k (a*a)_k = (\sum a)^2 = S^2$. And there are $2n-1$ values. So $\max \geq S^2/(2n-1)$.

Also, $(a*a)_0 = a_0^2$ and $(a*a)_{2n-2} = a_{n-1}^2$. These could be small.

For the score to be < 2, we need $2nM/S^2 < 2$, i.e., $M < S^2/n$.

Since $M \geq S^2/(2n-1)$, we need $S^2/(2n-1) \leq M < S^2/n$, which requires $2n-1 > n$, i.e., $n > 1$. Always true.

So it IS possible in principle. The question is what sequence achieves it.
