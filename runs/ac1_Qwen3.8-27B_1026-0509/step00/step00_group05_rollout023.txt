Δf_{n-1} ≈ -2*dx_i * x[n-1-i] + 2*dx_j * x[n-1-j] (first-order)

To reduce f_{n-1}, we want to decrease x at positions where x[n-1-i] is large, and increase x at positions where x[n-1-j] is small.

For the uniform, x[n-1-i] = 1/n for all i, so any move that preserves the sum will have zero first-order effect on f_{n-1}. We need second-order effects.

This suggests that the uniform is a "saddle point" or "flat" point, and we need a more aggressive perturbation to escape.

Alternative approach: Instead of gradient descent, use a different formulation.

Let me think about this problem from the perspective of the dual. The problem is:
minimize t
subject to: (x*x)[k] <= t for all k
             sum x = 1
             x >= 0

The Lagrangian is:
L = t + sum_k lambda_k ((x*x)[k] - t) - mu * (sum x - 1) - sum_i nu_i * x_i
= t(1 - sum_k lambda_k) + sum_k lambda_k (x*x)[k] - mu * sum x + mu - sum_i nu_i x_i

For the dual to be meaningful, we need 1 - sum_k lambda_k = 0, i.e., sum_k lambda_k = 1.

The dual function is: g(lambda, mu) = inf_x [sum_k lambda_k (x*x)[k] - mu * sum x + mu]
= sum_k lambda_k (x*x)[k] - mu + mu (assuming sum x = 1 at the optimum... this is getting circular).

Actually, let me not go down the dual path. Let me think of a more practical approach.

Key insight: The problem is equivalent to finding a probability distribution p on {0,...,n-1} that minimizes the peak of the distribution of X+Y where X, Y ~ p are i.i.d.

The optimal solution should be a distribution that makes X+Y as close to uniform on {0,...,2n-2} as possible.

One way to think about this: if p is uniform on {0,...,n-1}, then X+Y has a triangular distribution. The peak is at the center with value 1/n, and the tails have value 1/n^2.

To flatten the distribution of X+Y, we need to "push mass" from the center of the convolution toward the tails. This means we want p to be more "spread out" in some sense.

But p is already on the full support {0,...,n-1}! The issue is that the convolution of a full-support distribution always has a higher peak in the middle (due to the triangle inequality for熵 or something).

Actually, I recall that for the sum of two i.i.d. discrete random variables, the distribution is always "more peaked" than either individual. This is related to the fact that the Entropy Power Inequality says (E(X+Y))^2 >= 4 * E(X) * E(Y) in the continuous case, which implies that the "effective support" of X+Y is at least as large as that of X.

But we're not optimizing entropy; we're optimizing the peak. And the peak of X+Y can be made smaller than 1/n by using non-uniform p.
