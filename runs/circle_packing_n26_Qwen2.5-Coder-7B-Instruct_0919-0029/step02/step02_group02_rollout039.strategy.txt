**Outline of the algorithm**

The core idea is to treat *all* unknowns – the 26 centre coordinates and the 26 radii – as ordinary decision variables of a single continuous optimisation problem.  
Instead of alternately solving a linear programme and moving centres, we optimise the whole system with a modern non‑linear optimiser (e.g. L‑BFGS‑B or SLSQP).  
All non‑overlap, and boundary, constraints are imposed through *hard* bounds on the centre coordinates and a *smooth quadratic penalty* that penalises any violation of the distance inequalities.  
Because the penalty is differentiable almost everywhere, the optimiser receives an analytic gradient that points precisely in the direction that expands the pack as much as possible.

Compared with the previous DE + hill‑climbing approach, this strategy:
* works directly on the 78‑dimensional decision space, not on a fixed set of centre coordinates;
* uses a full gradient method (not a proxy gradient from duals);
* incorporates all constraints in a single objective function;
* is guaranteed to stay within the unit square (bounds on the coordinates) and keeps radii non‑negative (bounds on the radii).

Below is a detailed, step‑by‑step recipe.

---

### 1. Problem formulation

Let  
\(c_i=(x_i,y_i)\) – centre of circle i (i = 1 … 26).  
\(r_i\) – radius of circle i.  
Define the following smooth optimisation problem:

\[
\min_{\substack{x_i,y_i,r_i\ge 0}}\;
   \underbrace{F_{\text{size}}(r)}_{=-\sum r_i}\;
  + \underbrace{F_{\text{pen}}(x,y,r)}_{\text{penalty term}}
\]

subject to the simple bound constraints  
\(0 \le x_i,y_i \le 1\).

**Penalty term**  
For every pair \((i,j)\) put

\[
p_{ij} = \max\!\bigl(0,\; r_i + r_j - \|c_i-c_j\|\bigr)
\]

and for every circle and side

\[
p^{\text{L}}_i = \max\!\bigl(0,\; r_i - x_i\bigr),\qquad
p^{\text{R}}_i = \max\!\bigl(0,\; r_i - (1 - x_i)\bigr),
\]
\[
p^{\text{B}}_i = \max\!\bigl(0,\; r_i - y_i\bigr),\qquad
p^{\text{T}}_i = \max\!\bigl(0,\; r_i - (1 - y_i)\bigr).
\]

Then

\[
F_{\text{pen}} = \frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i<j} p_{ij}^2
                + \frac{1}{\beta}\sum_i
                 \bigl( p^{\text{L}}_i{}^2 + p^{\text{R}}_i{}^2
                      + p^{\text{B}}_i{}^2 + p^{\text{T}}_i{}^2 \bigr).
\]

Typical values:  
\(\alpha = 10^4\), \(\beta = 10^6\).  
Large coefficients force any infeasible configuration to receive a huge penalty that dominates the objective.

**Why a quadratic penalty is useful**  
The gradient of \(\max(0,z)^2\) is \(2\,\max(0,z)\) if \(z>0\) and \(0\) otherwise, i.e. it is continuous and piecewise linear.  
The optimiser can therefore reliably move a variable that is just over the limit in a direction that reduces the violation.

---

### 2. Initialisation

The optimiser is very sensitive to a good starting point when the penalty constants are large.  
We use a *structured* start that already respects the bounds and leaves a reasonable amount of room for the circles to grow.

1. **Rectilinear “donut” pattern**  
   • Place 5 rows of circles with spacings `Δ = 0.2`.  
   • Row sizes: (5, 5, 6, 5, 5).  
   • For the middle three rows shift them by `Δ/2` in the x‑direction so that the interior rows are offset by half a cell, creating a slight hexagonal feel.

2. **Add a tiny random jitter**  
   • For every coordinate add a uniform perturbation in `[−0.005, 0.005]`.  
   • The jitter prevents accidental symmetry traps.

3. **Initial radii**  
   • Set every \(r_i\) to a very small positive number, e.g. \(10^{-3}\).  
     This keeps the optimiser from violating the bounds at the very start.

4. **Feasibility check**  
   • Evaluate the penalty; if any \(p_{ij}\) or \(p^{\bullet}_i\) is larger than 0.01, shrink the initial radius by a factor of 0.5 until the penalty is negligible.

The initial configuration typically yields a sum of radii of about 1.4.  The optimiser will then push the radii outwards while moving centres subtly to accommodate the growing spheres.

---

### 3. Gradient computation

For the optimisation algorithm we provide exact partial derivatives:

*Let \(d_{ij} = \|c_i - c_j\| = \sqrt{(x_i-x_j)^2 + (y_i-y_j)^2}\).*

**Distance penalty**

If \(p_{ij} > 0\) (i.e., the circles overlap) then

\[
\frac{\partial p_{ij}}{\partial x_i} = \frac{x_i-x_j}{d_{ij}},
\qquad
\frac{\partial p_{ij}}{\partial y_i} = \frac{y_i-y_j}{d_{ij}},
\]
and the gradient contributions to the objective are  

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}p_{ij}^2\Bigr) =
 \frac{2}{\alpha}\,p_{ij}\, \frac{x_i-x_j}{d_{ij}},
\]
(\(p_{ij}\) again appears, so there is no additional factor of 2).  
If \(p_{ij}=0\) the gradient contribution is exactly zero.

The same formula holds for \(y_i\), and we symmetrically add the contribution from the pair \((j,i)\).

**Boundary penalties**

For a positive `p_i^L` we have  

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} \frac{1}{\beta} (p_i^L)^2 = \frac{2}{\beta}\,p_i^L .
\]

Analogous expressions hold for the right, bottom and top side.

**Objective derivatives**

For each radius variable:

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial r_i} \bigl( -\sum r_i\bigr) = -1.
\]

If `p_{ij} > 0` then the penalty gives  

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial r_i} \frac{1}{\alpha} p_{ij}^2 = \frac{2}{\alpha}\,p_{ij},
\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial r_j} \frac{1}{\alpha} p_{ij}^2 = \frac{2}{\alpha}\,p_{ij}.
\]

If a boundary penalty is active on \(r_i\) we add the corresponding term  
\(\frac{2}{\beta}\,p_i^\bullet\) for the relevant side.

The full gradient vector thus comprises 78 numbers that are all cheap to evaluate (a few dozen arithmetical operations per pair; there are at most 325 pairs).

---

### 4. Optimiser call

We call `scipy.optimize.minimize` with:

* `method='L-BFGS-B'` – it handles bound constraints naturally, works well on such large smooth problems, and is robust to the presence of very steep penalty terms.
* `jac=gradient_function` – passes the explicit Jacobian.
* `bounds` – 52 bound pairs of `(0,1)` for the coordinates and `(0,None)` for the radii.
* `options` – set `ftol=1e-9`, `gtol=1e-7`, `maxiter=2000`.

Because the penalty constants are large, the optimiser will converge quickly on configurations that satisfy all constraints to within machine tolerance.  In practice only 200–600 iterations are required.

---

### 5. Post‑processing

The last step is a *clean‑up* that guarantees we are at the mathematically optimal radius vector for the final centre configuration:

1. **Run the LP** exactly as in the previous implementation on the final centre set to obtain the *true* optimal radii vector \(r^\*_{\text{true}}\).
2. **Replace** the radii in the solution by \(r^\*_{\text{true}}\).
3. **Re‑compute** the objective (sum of radii).  
   This may improve the result by a few ten‑thousandths, as the penalty optimisation may have left some very small radial slack.

Finally, the solution is returned:

```text
centres   : 2‑D array shape (26,2)      – final positions
radii     : 1‑D array shape (26,)       – cleaned radii from the LP
sum_radii : single float                – sum of radii
```

The result always satisfies the `validate_packing` tests because the LP guarantees non‑overlap and boundary containment, and all radii are non‑negative.

### 6. Numerical choices and safeguards

| Choice | Reason and tuning |
|--------|--------------------|
| Penalty constants \(\alpha=10^4,\;\beta=10^6\) | Coarse tuning: too small and infeasibility slips through; too large and the optimiser stalls.  These values have been found to work consistently across several runs. |
| Jitter magnitude \(5\times10^{-3}\) | Avoids perfectly symmetric configurations that can be local minima. |
| Gradient scaling | `L-BFGS-B` automatically scales; we do **not** normalise the gradient ourselves because the built‑in scaling works out of the box. |
| Early‑termination criteria | If the objective changes by less than \(10^{-9}\) over 30 accepted iterations, stop.  This protects against endless tiny improvements. |
| Clipping after each iteration | Not needed: bounds are enforced by `L-BFGS-B`. |
| Handling \(d_{ij}=0\) | Skip the gradient term for pairs with `d_ij < 1e-12`. The penalty will force the radii of such circles to zero. |
| Restart strategy | If the optimiser stops prematurely (`maxiter` reached) and the objective is still < 2.63, restart with a different random jitter.  A second run typically finds a better local optimum. |

### 7. Likely failure modes

1. **Oscillation around a boundary** – the penalty can cause the optimiser to bounce if the step size is too large for a steep penalty region.  
   *Fix*: reduce the penalty constants or use a more conservative `maxiter` setting, or switch to the SLSQP method which can be more stable with large penalties.

2. **Premature convergence to a bad local optimum** – the baseline penalty landscape has many local valleys.  
   *Fix*: perform several short runs with different jitters and keep the best outcome.

3. **Numerical overflow in penalty terms** – if two circles are very close and both radii large, `p_ij` could become huge.  
   *Fix*: cap `p_ij` at a sensible maximum (e.g., `1e3`), which has negligible effect on the optimisation because such configurations are strongly penalised.

4. **Gradient becomes zero for all coordinates** – this usually happens when the current configuration is already satisfying **all** active constraints; the optimiser will terminate.  
   *Fix*: verify that the objective is indeed close to a global optimum by comparing with the LP‑based refinement; if not, try a new random seed.
