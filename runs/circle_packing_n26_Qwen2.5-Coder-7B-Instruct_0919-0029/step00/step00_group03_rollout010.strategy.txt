**High‑level idea**

Treat the packing as a *two‑step* process:

1. **Choose the circle centres** without fixing radii.  
   The centres are free points in the unit square that are constrained only by  
   the requirement that they leave *enough clearance* for a circle of some
   admissible radius.

2. **Given the centres, compute the largest possible radii** by solving a
   *linear* optimisation problem.  
   The LP guarantees the same configuration is perfectly feasible, while the
   optimiser in step 1 is free to use any continuous optimiser that only
   sees the geometric data (centres, distances to walls).

This decoupling yields a *convex inner problem* (the LP) that is solved
exactly, and a *convex–like outer problem* that is tackled by a *global
heuristic* (simulated annealing, particle swarm optimisation or a
simple greedy insertion).  The inner LP ensures the radii are always
optimal for the chosen centres, so the overall quality is limited only
by how well the centre configuration is positioned.

The approach is materially different from linear‑plus‑non‑linear
optimisation: it never tries to optimise radii **together** with
centres, and it uses a *stochastic* search for the centres instead of a
single deterministic SLSQP run.  This gives a new exploration
behaviour and a different risk of local minima, thereby offering a fresh
angle on the packing problem.

---

### 1.  Geometry‑preprocessing

* **Boundary distances.**  
  For any point \(p=(x,y)\) in the unit square, define  
  \[
  d_{\text{wall}}(p)=\min\{x,\;1-x,\;y,\;1-y\}.
  \]
  This is the greatest radius a circle centred at \(p\) can have *without
  touching the wall*.

* **Pairwise distances.**  
  For any two points \(p_i, p_j\) compute  
  \[
  d_{\text{pair}}(i,j)=\lVert p_i-p_j\rVert_2.
  \]
  These values are the only data needed besides the positions when the
  LP for radii is built.

All of the above can be computed on demand inside the optimiser, or
pre‑computed once the centres are fixed.

---

### 2.  Inner LP – “maximal radii for fixed centres”

Given the centres \(p_i\), the radii \(r_i\) are found by solving

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{maximize}   \quad & \sum_{i=1}^{26} r_i \\
\text{subject to} \quad & r_i \;\le\; d_{\text{wall}}(p_i) \qquad (4\;\text{constraints per circle}) \\
                        & r_i + r_j \;\le\; d_{\text{pair}}(i,j) \qquad (\text{one per pair}) \\
                        & r_i \;\ge\; 0 \qquad (26\;\text{constraints})
\end{aligned}
\]

All constraints are linear; the objective is linear.  The LP can be
solved quickly with `scipy.optimize.linprog` (HiGHS backend).  The
solution is **exactly** optimal radii for the fixed centres.

Because the LP is convex, it always converges and never produces an
invalid packing.  The only function of the outer optimiser is to
present *different* centre sets in order to maximise the LP objective.

---

### 3.  Outer optimiser – stochastic search over centres

We need a continuous optimiser that handles only the *geometric*
constraints \(p_i \in [0,1]^2\) and the *boundedness* of radii (which is
tested via the LP).  Several candidates exist; we choose a *hybrid
simulated annealing + greedy insertion* that balances exploitation and
exploration:

#### 3.1  Initial configuration

* Start with a sensible deterministic layout that already respects the
  wall constraints and offers a decent sum of radii:
  * 5 × 5 grid of centres at coordinates \((0.1+i\cdot0.2,\;0.1+j\cdot0.2)\) for \(i,j=0\ldots 4\).
  * One additional centre at the centre of the square \((0.5,0.5)\).

  All these positions lie inside the square and allow at least a radius
  of \(0.1\).  Compute the LP to get the initial radii and sum.

#### 3.2  Temperature schedule (Simulated Annealing)

* Let \(E(C) = -\) (sum of radii returned by the LP for centres \(C\)).
* Start at a high temperature \(T_0\) (e.g. 1.0) and decrease it
  geometrically: \(T_{k+1}= \alpha T_k\) with \(\alpha=0.995\).
* At each temperature, perform a fixed number of *generations* \(G\)
  (e.g. 2000).  In a generation:
  1. Pick a random circle index \(i\).
  2. Propose a new centre \(p_i'\) by adding a Gaussian perturbation  
     \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) to \(p_i\) (clamp to \([0,1]^2\) if needed).
  3. Replace \(p_i\) with \(p_i'\); recompute all pairwise distances
     involving \(i\) (cheap, \(O(n)\)).
  4. Solve the LP for the new set of centres.  Let the new sum of radii
     be \(S_{\text{new}}\).
  5. Accept the move with probability
     \[
     \min\!\left(1,\; \exp\!\bigl((S_{\text{new}}-S_{\text{old}})/T\bigr)\right).
     \]
     If accepted, keep the new arrangement; otherwise revert.
  6. Periodically (e.g. every 100 generations) perform *greedy
     gradient‑like* moves: push every circle a tiny step away from all
     neighbours that it is “close” to, until the LP improves.

* Stop when temperature drops below a small threshold (e.g. \(10^{-6}\))
  or when a fixed maximum number of objective evaluations is reached
  (e.g. 100 000).  Throughout, keep track of the best centre set seen.

#### 3.3  Constraints handling

Because the LP guarantees that every returned radius satisfies all
overlap and boundary constraints, we do **not** impose additional
non‑linear constraints in the outer loop.  The only meta‑constraint is
\(p_i\in[0,1]^2\) which is enforced by clamping after each perturbation
and by the bounds in the LP (inequalities \(r_i \le d_{\text{wall}}\)).

This simplification removes the need for tolerance‑handling during
simulated annealing – we let the LP do the strict feasibility checking.
If the LP fails (which shouldn't happen with clamped centres), we
revert the move.

### 4.  Numerical details and stability

* **LP precision.**  
  Use the high‑precision options of HiGHS (if available) or
  `method='highs'`.  This ensures the LP remains exact enough to
  satisfy the validator’s tolerance.

* **Random seeds.**  
  Initialise the random number generator with a fixed seed (e.g.
  `np.random.seed(42)`) so that the whole run is deterministic.

* **Step size schedule.**  
  The Gaussian perturbation standard deviation \(\sigma\) is reduced
  together with temperature so that early moves explore widely
  (\(\sigma=0.1\)) and later moves fine‑tune (\(\sigma=10^{-4}\)).

* **Early stopping.**  
  If during a temperature level no improvement in the best sum is
  observed after `G` generations, halve the temperature earlier to
  accelerate convergence.

### 5.  Failure modes and mitigations

| Failure mode                                   | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|------------------------------------------------|------------|-------------|
| LP infeasible due to numerical round‑off      | Very low   | Re‑execute LP with slightly relaxed bound (`d_wall - 1e-12`) or simply clip radii to the feasible set if solver returns `infeasible`. |
| SA chain stuck in a local optimum             | Medium     | Restart from a different initial perturbation (e.g. a random grid or a single‑circle expanded configuration). |
| Very small improvements at low temperatures   | Medium     | Increase the number of generations per temperature or add occasional *jumps* (e.g. resample a few centres uniformly). |
| Over‑large circles being clipped by walls in LP | Low        | The LP constraints enforce \(r_i \le d_{\text{wall}}\); no violation can occur. |
| Validation fails due to floating‑point errors | Low        | After optimisation, clip any negative radii to zero and scale all radii by a factor \(0.9999999\); this guarantees compatibility with the validator while changing the sum by < 1e‑6. |
