**Goal** – Produce a step function  
\(h:[0,2]\rightarrow[0,1]\) (discretised with \(n\) samples) that minimises  

\[
C_{5}(h)=\max_{k}\;\int_{0}^{2} h(x)\bigl(1-h(x+k)\bigr)\,dx .
\]

The discretised version (used by the evaluator) is  

\[
C_{5}(h)=\max_{s\in\{-n+1,\dots ,n-1\}}
\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+s}\bigr)\Bigr)\,dx ,
\qquad
dx=\frac{2}{n},
\]

with the conventions that indices outside \([0,n-1]\) are taken as 0.  
Constraints are  

* \(0\le h_i\le 1\) for all \(i\)  
* \(\sum_i h_i\,dx =1\;\Longleftrightarrow\; \sum_i h_i = n/2\).

The optimisation is a **min‑max quadratic** problem because each term contains the product
\(h_i\,h_{i+s}\).  A direct non‑linear search works but converges slowly; a **convex‑relaxation + integer‑refinement** pipeline gives a far more systematic and usually superior result while staying well within the 600 s budget.

### 1. Reformulation as a linear programme with McCormick envelopes
Write the correlation for a shift \(s\) as  

\[
C_s = \underbrace{\sum_i h_i}_{=\,n/2} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum_i h_i\,h_{i+s}}_{=:Q_s}.
\]

Thus  

\[
C_{5}(h)=\max_s\;\bigl(\tfrac{n}{2}-Q_s\bigr)
       =\tfrac{n}{2}-\min_s Q_s .
\]

Minimising \(C_{5}\)  ⇔   **maximising** the smallest autocorrelation value \(\min_s Q_s\).

Introduce an auxiliary scalar \(t\) that upper‑bounds every correlation:

\[
\max_s C_s \le t
\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
\forall s:\; \tfrac{n}{2}\;-\;Q_s \;\le\; t
\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
Q_s \;\ge\; \tfrac{n}{2}-t .
\tag{1}
\]

The only non‑linear terms are the products \(h_i h_{i+s}\).  For each pair
\((i,s)\) define a new variable  

\[
y_{i,s}\; \approx\; h_i\,h_{i+s}\qquad
(0\le i<n,\;0\le i+s<n).
\]

The *McCormick envelope* gives the exact convex hull of the bilinear term when
\(h_i\) and \(h_{i+s}\) are bounded in \([0,1]\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
y_{i,s} &\le h_i,                     &
y_{i,s} &\le h_{i+s},\\
y_{i,s} &\ge h_i+h_{i+s}-1,           &
y_{i,s} &\ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

All constraints (2) are linear.  Substituting \(Q_s=\sum_i y_{i,s}\) in (1) yields a **pure linear programme**:

* **Variables** – \(h_i\;(0\le i<n)\),  \(y_{i,s}\) for all admissible \((i,s)\), and the scalar \(t\).
* **Objective** – minimise \(t\).
* **Constraints**
  * \(0\le h_i\le 1\)   (box)
  * \(\sum_i h_i = n/2\) (mass)
  * \(t\ge 0\) (since correlations are non‑negative)
  * McCormick constraints (2) for each \((i,s)\)
  * For every shift \(s\): \(\displaystyle \sum_i y_{i,s} \ge \tfrac{n}{2}-t\).

All are linear; the problem size is modest:
for \(n=70\) there are ≈ 2450 \(y\)-variables and ≈ 140 shift constraints.
An LP of this size solves in a fraction of a second with a modern interior‑point
solver (ECOS, OSQP, or the CBC‑based LP mode of cvxpy).

### 4. Local polishing of a binary candidate (hill‑climbing)

The rounded vector is often already close to optimal, but a tiny amount of
post‑processing can push the maximal correlation below the current record.
Use a **targeted swap heuristic** that focuses on the shift that presently attains the maximum.

**Data needed for a given binary vector**  
For every shift \(s\) compute  
\(C_s = \sum_i h_i\bigl(1-h_{i+s}\bigr)\) (the same as the evaluator does).  
Let \(s_{\max}\) be an arg‑max of \(C_s\).

**Swap move**  
* Find a *source* index \(i\) such that \(h_i=1\) **and** \(h_{i+s_{\max}}=0\) – this contributes 1 to the worst correlation.  
* Find a *target* index \(j\) such that \(h_j=0\) **and** \(h_{j+s_{\max}}=1\) – swapping would remove a 1 from a bad place and add a 1 where it is already matched.  
* Swapping \(h_i\) and \(h_j\) reduces \(C_{s_{\max}}\) by 2 (or by 1 if one of the two shifted partners lies outside the overlap region).  

**Greedy iteration**
1. Scan all mismatched source–target pairs for the current worst shift and pick the pair giving the largest reduction in the *global* maximum (i.e. recompute the whole correlation after a hypothetical swap; keep the best).
2. Perform the chosen swap if it strictly lowers the global max; otherwise stop the local search for this candidate.
3. Update the worst shift and repeat until no improving swap exists or time runs out.

Because each swap involves only two indices, the cost of recomputing the
correlation can be reduced to \(O(n)\) using incremental updates,
but with \(n=70\) a full recomputation with `np.correlate` is still negligible.
