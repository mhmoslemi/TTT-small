assistantfinal strategy**Outline of a new, fundamentally different algorithm**

The previous attempt treated the problem as a pure “set‑of‑equations” optimization: the whole 52‑dimensional vector was fed to a nonlinear solver that had to discover *simultaneously* both the positions and the radii.  
The key idea of this new approach is to **separate the two variables** and supply a *deterministic, physics‑inspired driving force* for the positions:

1. **Positions are updated by following the “tightest” constraint:**  
   a circle will always try to move in the direction that enlarges the currently most restrictive distance (to a wall or to a neighbour).  
   This turns the search in the position space into a very cheap “gradient‑like” ascent, while the radii are still obtained by a *linear* optimisation problem that is exact for any fixed set of centers.

2. **Radii remain linear**: once the centre coordinates are fixed, the largest feasible radii are those that solve a linear program (see below).  
   This guarantees that each iteration produces a **feasible** configuration, so we never have to recover from overlapping circles.

3. **Iterative refinement loop**: we run the gradient‑like mover for a fixed number of iterations, then record the best sum of radii.  
   If stagnation occurs we restart with a new random (or lattice‑based) centre set.  
   By pair‑wise “bidirectional” movement of touching circles we avoid the excessive oscillations that a naive one‑side mover would generate.

4. **Optional simulated‑annealing escape**: when a restart is performed, we allow a small stochastic perturbation (Gaussian noise with vanishing variance) after every couple of gradient steps.  
   This gives the algorithm the ability to escape shallow local optima without introducing the full overhead of a complete SA loop.

5. **Final SLSQP polishing**: the best solution found by the iterative loop is re‑optimised with the *non‑linear* SLSQP routine to correct any tiny residual violations (the LP guarantees feasibility up to numerical tolerances, but the SLSQP fine‑tuning can squeeze the radii a little further).

**Mathematical formulation**

*For a fixed centre configuration \(C=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{26}\):*  

- **Boundary constraints**  
  \[
  r_i \le x_i,\quad r_i \le 1-x_i,\quad r_i \le y_i,\quad r_i \le 1-y_i.
  \]
- **Pairwise constraints**  
  \[
  r_i + r_j \le d_{ij},\quad d_{ij}=\sqrt{(x_i-x_j)^2+(y_i-y_j)^2}.
  \]

The   *maximise* problem \(\max \sum_i r_i\) is then a **linear program** with variables \(r_1,\dots,r_{26}\).  
The LP has exactly \(26+26+ (26\times25)/2 = 354\) inequalities (≈ 354 constraints) and 26 variables; it is solved in microseconds by the “highs” solver in SciPy.

*Active constraint identification*  
After solving the LP, for each circle \(i\) we find the index \(k_i\) that achieves the minimal value in the inequalities above (with a small tolerance to decide between parallel walls or a neighbour).  
That active constraint determines the *direction of motion* for the next step.

*Gradient‑like update*  
Let the step size be \(\alpha\) (initially \(\alpha=0.01\), halved every 20 iterations).  
For a wall active constraint:  

- left wall → \((\Delta x,\Delta y) = (\alpha, 0)\)  
- right wall → \((-\alpha, 0)\)  
- bottom → \((0,\alpha)\)  
- top → \((0,-\alpha)\)

For a neighbour constraint (touching circle \(j\)):  

- \(\mathbf{u}_{ij} = \frac{(x_i-x_j,\; y_i-y_j)}{d_{ij}}\)  
- \((\Delta x,\Delta y) = \alpha \,\mathbf{u}_{ij}\)

The new tentative centre is  
\[
(x_i',y_i') = (x_i,y_i) + (\Delta x,\Delta y).
\]
If this moves the circle outside the unit square we clip to the boundary.  
After all circles are updated simultaneously we recompute the LP to obtain the new radii.  
We accept the move if the sum of radii increased by at least \(10^{-7}\); otherwise we keep the previous centres (this simple acceptance rule keeps the walk monotone).

*Bidirectional refinement* – optional: whenever two circles are touching (i.e. their active constraint is between them), we move *both* circles simultaneously in opposite directions using the same \(\alpha\).  
That keeps the pair of distances roughly constant while enabling the circles to shift into a better local configuration (preventing the situation where a circle keeps “pushing” on a neighbour that cannot move).

**Algorithmic steps**

1. **Generate initial centre sets**  
   - **Lattice‑based seed**: place points on a hexagonal lattice that approximately fits inside the square (e.g. \(5\times5\) with staggered rows, then add a few extra points near the centre or edges).  
   - **Random seed**: pick 26 points uniformly at random in \([0,1]^2\).  
   We will run the entire iterative loop for each seed, keeping the best result.

2. **Iterative position‑radius loop (per seed)**  
   1. Solve LP for the current centres → radii.  
   2. For `iteration = 0 … max_iter-1`:  
      a. Identify active constraints for all circles.  
      b. Compute tentative new centres using the rules above (with current \(\alpha\)).  
      c. Clip to the square.  
      d. Solve LP again → new radii.  
      e. If sum of radii improved: accept the tentative centres, otherwise revert.  
      f. Reduce \(\alpha\) every `cooling_interval` iterations.  
      g. If fewer than `accept_rate` circles improved or the total sum increased by < `epsilon` for `patience` consecutive iterations, break early (convergence).  
   3. Record the best sum of radii reached during this seed run.

3. **Random perturbation escape**  
   After every `escape_interval` iterations (e.g. 50 iterations) we add a small Gaussian perturbation to all centre coordinates (variance proportional to current \(\alpha\)).  
   This stochastic element allows exploration of neighbouring basins of attraction without full random restarts.

4. **Select global best**  
   After all seeds are processed, keep the configuration yielding the largest sum of radii.

5. **Fine‑tuning with SLSQP**  
   Use the best centres and radii as an initial guess for a full‐scale nonlinear optimisation:  
   ```
   minimize   f(centers, radii) = -sum(radii)
   constraints: all boundary and pairwise non‑overlap inequalities (type 'ineq')
   bounds for centres: [0,1], for radii: [0,0.5]
   ```
   The SLSQP run is kept short (maxiter≈2000) because the starting point is already near feasibility.

6. **Validation**  
   Run the provided `validate_packing` routine.  
   If it fails, we simply raise the exception; the failure will point unambiguously to the offending pair or boundary conflict.

**Anticipated failure modes and mitigations**

1. **Stagnation in a local optimum**  
   *Mitigation*: bidirectional moves help; if no improvement after `patience` iterations we force a random perturbation.

2. **Oscillations near a wall**  
   The direction‐wise step rule ensures motion is always *away* from the active wall, so it cannot oscillate unless the wall becomes inactive. In that case we continue moving until the next active wall is reached.

3. **Numerical drift (circles just touching)**  
   LP guarantees feasibility up to machine precision; however we clip positions to the box before solving, preventing a centre from drifting outside.

4. **Large radii causing wall collisions after bidirectional shifts**  
   We clip after every update and redo the LP. The LP cannot increase a radius beyond the distance to a wall, so even if the centre is clipped to exactly a wall, the LP will reduce that radius to zero – a very rare event.

5. **Too many iterations**  
   We set a hard limit of 200 iterations per seed; in practice convergence occurs earlier. The overall runtime remains well below the 1‑CPU limit.

**Why this strategy should beat the previous one**

- The gradient‑like mover is *exactly* guided by the most restrictive constraint, i.e. the geometry that actually limits the sum of radii.  
- Unlike random SA, every iteration has a deterministic, monotonic improvement, so we can make more progress per LP solve.  
- The bidirectional update preserves pairwise distances, keeping the contact graph stable and avoiding unnecessary back‑and‑forth movement.  
- The final SLSQP does only a short polish, which captures any subtle nonlinear relationships that the LP may have missed.  
- Because the LP is solved at every iteration, the algorithm automatically enforces global feasibility; we never leave the feasible set.
