The goal is to maximize  

\[
S \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{26} r_i
\]

subject to

*  \(0 \le x_i, y_i \le 1\)  
*  \(r_i \ge 0\)  
*  \(x_i-r_i \ge 0,\;x_i+r_i \le 1,\;y_i-r_i \ge 0,\;y_i+r_i \le 1\) (**boundary**)
*  \(\| (x_i,y_i) -(x_j,y_j) \|_2 \;\ge\; r_i+r_j\) for \(i\neq j\) (**non‑overlap**)

With 26 circles the optimal packing is highly non‑trivial.  
The following plan blends analytic insight (hexagonal filling,
corner‑touching, nested groups) with a robust numerical search that
seeks an improved sum of radii.

### 1. Analytic ideas – why the “canonical” layout is suboptimal

* **Hexagonal lattice**  
  In the infinite plane the densest circle packing is hexagonal, giving a
  packing fraction of \( \pi/(2\sqrt{3})\approx0.9069\).  Inside a unit square,
  truncating this lattice near the boundary loses area: circles must shrink
  to fit in the corners, compromising the overall sum of radii.  Thus a pure
  hexagonal pattern is unlikely to attain the best sum.

* **Corner and edge circles**  
  A circle that is tangent to two adjacent sides can have radius up to
  \(\frac{1}{2}\) (center at \((0.5,0.5)\) touches left/right/top/bottom).
  The best packing tends to place large circles at the four corners, each
  touching two sides.  In a unit square the largest radius that keeps the
  circle inside is \(r=\min(0.5,\,0.5)=0.5\).  
  If we place all four corners at radius 0.5, the remaining area is very
  small, so we must reduce the corner radii a bit to allow more circles in
  the inner region.  Search over a “corner radius parameter” \(r_c\in[0.3,0.5]\)
  is a natural first step.

* **Inner “hexagonal core”**  
  After carving out the four corner circles we can fill the central
  rectangle with a compact hexagonal network.  The centers can be placed
  on a 3×4 or 4×4 grid with equal spacing \(d\) that depends on the
  remaining clearance.  Typically the optimal layout uses 18–20 inner
  circles, each about 0.08–0.1 in radius.  Fine‑tuning the spacing
  yields a better \(S\).

* **Layered scaling**  
  Empirically, the optimal configuration consists of two layers:
  * outer layer: 6 circles (4 corners + 2 mid‑edge circles)
  * inner layer: 20 circles arranged hexagonally.

  Scaling each layer independently (share a common radius within a
  layer) introduces only a handful of variables while preserving the
  essential geometry.

### 2. Numerical approach – constrained optimization

Because the problem is highly non‑convex, we use a two‑stage strategy:

#### 2.1. Structured “seed” placement

Create a deterministic seed that satisfies all constraints and has a
reasonable \(S\).  The seed is produced as follows:

1. **Corner circles**  
   Parameter \(r_c\) – start with \(r_c=0.45\).  
   Centers:  
   \((r_c,r_c),\, (1-r_c,r_c),\, (r_c,1-r_c),\, (1-r_c,1-r_c)\).

2. **Edge circles**  
   Parameter \(r_e\).  
   Centers at the mid‑points of the four edges, e.g. \((0.5, r_e)\), etc.  
   Choose \(r_e\) so that the edge circle touches both adjacent corner
   circles:  
   \[
   \sqrt{(0.5-r_c)^2 + (r_c-r_e)^2} = r_c + r_e
   \]
   Solve this equation for \(r_e\).

3. **Inner hexagonal core**  
   Parameter \(r_i\) (common radius of all inner circles).  
   Generate a hexagonal grid inside the square, remove positions that
   lie outside the allowed region (i.e. intersecting the outer
   circles).  The grid spacing is \(d=2\sqrt{3}\,r_i\).  
   Adjust \(r_i\) so that the innermost inner circle touches its nearest
   neighbor and is tangent to the surrounding larger circles (corners or
   edges).  This gives a relation between \(r_i\) and \(r_c,r_e\).

The seed will have at most **three free parameters** \((r_c,r_e,r_i)\).
We can evaluate \(S\) and all constraints for any choice of these
parameters very fast.

#### 2.2. Global optimization over the three parameters

Use **SciPy’s differential evolution** (`scipy.optimize.differential_evolution`)
to maximize \(S\) subject to the constraint that:

* All circles stay inside the square (automatically satisfied by construction).
* No overlap: the only possible overlaps are between inner circles and
  adjacent outer circles, or among inner neighbors.  The relations
  derived in the seed step ensure tangency; we leave a small hysteresis
  margin to guard against floating‑point errors.

The search bounds are:

```
r_c ∈ [0.30, 0.49]
r_e ∈ [0.10, 0.25]
r_i ∈ [0.05, 0.15]
```

The objective function returns `-S` because differential evolution
minimizes.

#### 2.3. Fine‑grained local refinement

After DE finishes we do a short **Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP)**
run (`scipy.optimize.minimize` with method='SLSQP') starting from the DE
solution, treating the remaining 26 variables (`x_i,y_i,r_i`) as free
variables.  We add the usual nonlinear constraints (boundary and
non‑overlap) directly to SLSQP.  This local phase can adjust tiny shifts
and radii, potentially increasing \(S\) by a few thousandths.

### 4. Dealing with failure modes

* **DE stuck at boundary** – enlarge the parameter ranges, or
  reinitialize using random seeds (`init='latinhypercube'`).

* **SLSQP fails to converge** – increase `maxiter`, lower `ftol`, or
  relax the non‑overlap tolerance slightly (`-1e-8`) while checking
  with `validate_packing`.

* **Floating‑point overflow** – all radii are ≤0.5; distances are
  ≤√2, so double precision is more than enough.

* **Duplicate/identical solutions** – because the problem is symmetric,
  we accept any optimal packing.  The validation only checks feasibility,
  not uniqueness.
