## Approach and Rationale

The central idea for improving the upper bound on the Erdős minimum overlap constant is to use a multi-objective evolutionary optimization (MOEO) algorithm combined with automatic differentiation for gradient-based refinement. This approach differs from the local gradient-based optimization in that it globally explores the function space while leveraging gradient information for faster convergence and more robust solutions. The key rationale is that the original optimization strategy might be overly reliant on local gradients, potentially missing the global optimum if trapped in a suboptimal region. By using evolutionary methods, we can better explore the high-dimensional space and incorporate symmetry, which may lead to a function with more harmonic behavior or reduced maximum overlap.

The bottleneck addressed is the potential for local minima in unconstrained spaces. To counter this, the algorithm introduces random mutations and crossovers, ensuring diverse sampling. The gradient refinement step allows efficient local optimization once promising regions are found. For the Erdős problem, this can lead to a more periodic or fractal-like shape optimizing the autocorrelation minima.

Important limitations include the computational cost of gradient evaluation (requiring O(n_points^2) per evaluation) and the risk of premature convergence if hyperparameters are not tuned. This strategy is justified because it is a novel structure affecting how we explore the space, going beyond simple restarts or stochastically perturbed local optimization in the adjacent plans.

## Mathematical Specification

### Variables and Representation
We represent the step function \( h: [0,2] \rightarrow [0,1] \) as a discrete vector \( \mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{points}}} \), with sample points spaced at \( dx = 2.0 / n_{\text{points}} \). The vector is symmetric around \( x=1 \) to exploit known properties:  
\[ h[i] = h[\text{len}(h) - 1 - i] \]  
for all \( i \). The constraint \( \int h(x) \, dx = 1 \) is enforced by:  
\[ \sum_{i=0}^{n_{\text{points}}-1} h[i] \cdot dx = 1 \]  
Since \( dx = 2.0 / n_{\text{points}} \), this simplifies to:  
\[ \sum_{i=0}^{n_{\text{points}}-1} h[i] = \frac{n_{\text{points}}}{2} \]  
All \( h[i] \) satisfy \( 0 \leq h[i] \leq 1 \).

The objective to minimize is \( C_5 = \max_k \int_{0}^{2} h(x) \cdot (1 - h(x+k)) \, dx \). In discretized form:  
\[ C_5 \coloneqq \max_{k=-289}^{289} \sum_{i=0}^{279} h[i] \cdot (1 - h[(i+k) \mod 280]) \cdot dx \]  
where indices modulo \( n_{\text{points}} = 280 \) are used, as this corresponds to circular correlation (mode full is evaluated last for symmetry). However, for strict feasibility, we use symmetric implementation.

The mathematical equivalence to maximizing the minimum autocorrelation is valid under the finite domain:  
\[ I_k \coloneqq \int_{0}^{2} h(x) \cdot h(x+k) \, dx \]  
\[ C_k = \int_{0}^{2} h(x) \cdot (1 - h(x+k)) \, dx = 1 - I_k \]  
At optimum, \( \sum h[i] dx = 1 \), so \( C_5 = \max_k (1 - I_k) = 1 - \min_k I_k \).

Independent max and min references clarify:  
- \( C_5 \) is the target minimization.  
- Maximizing \( \min_k I_k \) indirectly minimizes \( C_5 \).
