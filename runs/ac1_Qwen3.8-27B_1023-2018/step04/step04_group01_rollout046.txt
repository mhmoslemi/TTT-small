Actually, let me think about this differently. The LP approach is essentially:
- At each iteration, solve a linear constraint relaxation
- The sequence of LP solutions defines a polytope
- We're looking for a point in the intersection of {f>=0, sum(f)=1} with {max(f*f) minimized}

This is similar to the Demidenko-Khachiyan similarity scheme or Frank-Wolfe (conditional gradient) methods.

In Frank-Wolfe:
- min f(x) s.t. x in S (convex)
- At each step: solve min_{x in S} <∇f(x_k), x - x_k>, then update x = (1-t)x_k + t*s

Here, S = {x >= 0, sum(x) = 1} (simplex), and f(x) = max_k (x*x)[k].

The subgradient of f at x is: ∇f(x) = 2 * (p * x_rev) where p is the subgradient of max (uniform over argmax, or softmax for smoothing).

So Frank-Wolfe would be:
1. Compute subgradient g = 2*(p * x_rev)[:n] (the gradient of max(f*f))
2. Solve min_{y in simplex} <g, y> = argmin_y sum_j g_j * y_j s.t. y>=0, sum(y)=1
   This is just y = e_min (put all mass on the smallest g component)
3. Update: x = (1-t)*x + t*e_min

But this is crude. The LP approach is better because it considers the full constraint structure.

Let me focus on making the LP approach more effective:

1. **Tighten the constraint**: Instead of (f*g) <= M, use (f*g) <= M * (1 - epsilon) for small epsilon. This forces g to be "opposite" to f in some sense.

2. **Multi-LP**: Solve LPs with different constraint subsets to get diverse directions.

3. **Warm start**: Use the previous LP solution as a warm start for the next LP.

4. **Adaptive step**: Instead of fixed binary search, use the LP solution to determine a natural step size.
