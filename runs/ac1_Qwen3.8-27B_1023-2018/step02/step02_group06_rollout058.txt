But let me think about whether there are even better shapes. The issue with the polynomial ramp is that (a*a)[n-1] = a · a_rev is determined by the "overlap" of a with its reverse. To minimize this, we want a to be very different from its reverse.

The extreme case: a = [ε, ε, ..., ε, 1, 1, ..., 1] (small on the left, large on the right). Then a_rev = [1, 1, ..., 1, ε, ε, ..., ε] and the inner product a · a_rev is small (cross terms are ε*1 or 1*ε).

But wait, the convolution at other lags might be large. For example, (a*a)[n-1] = sum a_j * a_{n-1-j}. If a = [ε, 1] (n=2):
(a*a)[0] = ε²
(a*a)[1] = 2ε
(a*a)[2] = 1
max = 1, sum = ε+1, score = 4*1/(ε+1)² ≈ 4 for small ε. Bad!

For n=2, the best is uniform: a = [1/2, 1/2], score = 4*(1/2)/1 = 2.

The problem is that for short sequences, the "edge" terms (a[0]², a[n-1]², etc.) matter a lot. For long sequences, the middle term dominates.

For the polynomial ramp of length n, the score approaches 2(p+1)²(p!)²/(2p+1)! as n→∞. This is the theoretical best for this family.

Can we beat the polynomial ramp? In principle, yes - by designing a sequence where a · a_rev is even smaller relative to (sum a)², while keeping all other convolution terms small.

But for our purposes, the polynomial ramp with p=2 or p=3 already gives scores well below the target. Let me just go with that.
