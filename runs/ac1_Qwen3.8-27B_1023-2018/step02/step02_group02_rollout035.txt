Let me think about the golden section line search more carefully.

Given f and g (both sum to 1), define h(t) = (1-t)f + t*g.
The objective is M(t) = max_k (h*h)[k].

Since h = (1-t)f + t*g:
(h*h)[k] = sum_j h[j] * h[k-j]
= sum_j [(1-t)f[j] + t*g[j]] * [(1-t)f[k-j] + t*g[k-j]]
= (1-t)^2 (f*f)[k] + (1-t)t [(f*g)[k] + (g*f)[k]] + t^2 (g*g)[k]

Note that (f*g)[k] = (g*f)[k] (convolution is commutative for 1D), so:
(h*h)[k] = (1-t)^2 A[k] + 2(1-t)t B[k] + t^2 C[k]
where A = f*f, B = f*g, C = g*g.

So M(t) = max_k [(1-t)^2 A[k] + 2(1-t)t B[k] + t^2 C[k]]

This is the max of quadratics in t, hence convex. We can minimize it with golden section search.

Pre-computing A, B, C costs 3 FFT convolutions, then the line search is just evaluating max of pre-computed quadratics. Very fast!

**Even better**: We can find the exact minimum of M(t) analytically. The minimum occurs where:
- Either at t=0 or t=1 (boundary)
- Or at a point where two quadratics are equal and their derivatives point away (a "kink" in M(t))
- Or at a point where one quadratic has its minimum and is the maximum

For the second case, we need to find pairs (i,j) such that q_i(t) = q_j(t) and q_i(t) >= q_k(t) for all k, and the derivative of q_i is negative while q_j's is positive (or vice versa).

The LP iteration is:

2. Solve LP: max sum(g) s.t. (f*g) <= M, g >= 0
3. Update f = (1-t)f + t*g with optimal t
