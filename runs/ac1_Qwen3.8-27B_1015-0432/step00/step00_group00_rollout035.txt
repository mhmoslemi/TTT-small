Let me try yet another approach. The key insight from harmonic analysis:

For a nonnegative sequence a with sum S, the convolution (a*a) has sum S^2 and is "concentrated" at the center. The ratio max/sum is minimized when the convolution is as flat as possible.

One way to flatten it: make a have "cancellations" in its frequency domain. But since a ≥ 0, we can't have negative values. However, we can have a rapidly varying (but nonnegative) sequence.

I think the fundamental issue is that for nonnegative sequences, the convolution peak is at the center and is approximately S^2/n (by some form of the uncertainty principle or rearrangement inequality). To beat this significantly, we need the sequence to be structured in a way that the "energy" at the center lags is reduced.

The 2-block structure works because it creates a "step" that reduces the center convolution. The value at the center becomes proportional to A*B (cross term) rather than A^2.

For a more general approach, let me think about partitioning into blocks. If we have m blocks of size n/m, with block values v_0, v_1, ..., v_{m-1}:

The center convolution (approximately) is: sum_i a_i * a_{n-1-i} ≈ (n/m) * sum_j v_j * v_{m-1-j} (by symmetry of the pairing).

The "block autocorrelation" max is: max_s sum_j v_j * v_{j+s} (like the convolution of the block values).

And the "within-block" contribution is: (n/m) * max_j v_j^2.

For the overall max to be small, we need both the block autocorrelation and the within-block self-correlation to be small.

If we use m blocks and the block values form a "good" autocorrelation sequence (like a Barker sequence or m-ary sequence), we can minimize the block-level max.
