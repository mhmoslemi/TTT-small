The key insight is: for large n, the "effective" constraint is on the middle of the convolution (indices around n-1), because that's where there are the most terms. The edges (indices 0 and 2n-2) only have 1 term each.

For the middle: c[n-1] = sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a[i]*a[n-1-i].

We want this to be small relative to S^2. If we think of a as a probability distribution (normalized to sum 1), then c[n-1] = sum a[i]*a[n-1-i] is like the "self-convolution at the center."

For a uniform distribution on {0, ..., n-1}, c[n-1] = sum (1/n)*(1/n) = n*(1/n^2) = 1/n. And S^2 = 1 (normalized). So the ratio c[n-1]/S^2 = 1/n, and score = 2n*(1/n) = 2.

To get below 2, we need c[n-1]/S^2 < 1/n, i.e., the "center" of the convolution should be less than 1/n of the total squared.

This means we want a[i]*a[n-1-i] to be "anti-correlated" - when a[i] is large, a[n-1-i] should be small.

A natural choice: a[i] = i+1 (linearly increasing) or a[i] = exp(i) (exponentially increasing).

To minimize the max, we want all c[k] to be as equal as possible. This is the "flat convolution" problem.

One approach: use a sequence that's symmetric (a[i] = a[n-1-i]). Then c[k] = c[2n-2-k], which reduces the search space.

For a symmetric sequence, the "center" of the convolution is at k = n-1, and by symmetry, c[n-1-k] = c[n-1+k]. The values c[0], c[1], ..., c[n-1] need to be minimized in max.
