The convolution at lag k is: sum_i p[i] * p[k-i] (for valid i).

The autocorrelation is always maximized at lag 0 (by Cauchy-Schwarz: sum p[i]p[i+k] <= sqrt(sum p[i]² * sum p[i+k]²) = sum p[i]² for k=0... actually that's not quite right either).

The convolution can have its maximum at different lags depending on the sequence structure.

Here's the key: for the convolution, the "peaks" don't have to be at the center. If the sequence has a "bump" at position i and a "bump" at position j, the convolution will have a peak at position i+j.

So by placing mass at specific positions, we can control WHERE the convolution peaks occur. The strategy is to distribute the mass so that the convolution peaks are as low as possible.

This is like a "packing" problem: place n "weights" (the p_i values, summing to 1) on {0, 1, ..., n-1} such that the maximum "pair sum" (p_i * p_j for i+j = k, summed over all pairs) is minimized.

For the uniform distribution, every pair (i, j) contributes equally, and the center lag (n-1) has the most pairs (n pairs), giving the highest value.

To reduce the center peak, we can "move" mass from the center to the edges. This reduces the number of pairs that sum to the center lag.

For example, if p is concentrated at the edges (positions 0 and n-1), the convolution has peaks at 0, n-1, and 2n-2. The center peak (n-1) comes from the cross-term p[0]*p[n-1] + p[n-1]*p[0] = 2*p[0]*p[n-1], which is small if the edge masses are small.

But the peaks at 0 and 2n-2 are p[0]² and p[n-1]², which are large if the edge masses are large.

So there's a trade-off. The optimal solution balances the peaks at all lags.

This is exactly the convex optimization problem I formulated earlier! And since it's convex on the non-negative orthant, we can solve it (at least approximately) with gradient methods.
