**Overall Idea**

1.  The sum of radii for any fixed set of circle centres is the solution of a small linear programme (LP).  
    *Objective*: maximise ∑ rᵢ  
    *Constraints*:  
    * 0 ≤ rᵢ ≤ bᵢ (where bᵢ = min(xᵢ, 1−xᵢ, yᵢ, 1−yᵢ) – boundary limit)  
    * rᵢ + rⱼ ≤ dᵢⱼ (no‑overlap, dᵢⱼ is the Euclidean distance between centres i and j).  

    With the HiGHS solver this LP is solved in a few milliseconds for 26 variables.

2.  Instead of moving the 26 centres continuously, we **search over discrete candidate sets** of centres.  
    *Generate a pool of roughly 50–60 candidate points inside the square (excluding the four exact corners, because a circle centred there would have radius 0).  Each candidate also keeps its boundary radius bᵢ as a quick estimate of the maximum it can possibly attain.*  
    *Include the central point (0.5, 0.5) in the pool; this guarantees a large circle of radius 0.5 in the final packing.*  

3.  **Greedy‑plus‑swap refinement**  
    *Initial selection*: pick the 26 candidates with the largest boundary radius bᵢ.  
    *Iterative swap*: while an improvement is found, attempt every possible swap between an unselected candidate and a selected one.  
      For each swap evaluate the LP for the new set of 26 centres, compute the resulting sum of radii, and keep the swap that yields the greatest improvement.  
      Because each iteration evaluates at most 26 × (unselected‑size) LPs, the cost is modest (≈ 600 LP solves per iteration).  Usually convergence is reached in fewer than 10 iterations.  

4.  **Fine‑tuning with local random perturbations**  
    After the discrete set stabilises, run a short simulated‑annealing or random‑walk over the 26 centres themselves (step sizes ∼ 10⁻² to 10⁻⁴, temperatures 1 → 10⁻⁴) while keeping the LP evaluation as the objective.  
    This allows the centres to drift slightly from the original discrete positions, potentially enlarging some radii that were limited by the discrete geometry.

5.  **Algorithmic Flow**

    1. **Candidate generation**  
       - Sample ~ 45 random points uniform on [0,1]² with the constraint min(x,1‑x,y,1‑y) ≥ 0.03 (ensures at least a tiny feasible radius).  
       - Add the central point (0.5, 0.5).  
       - Compute and keep the boundary radius bᵢ for each candidate.

    2. **Set construction**  
       - Sort candidates by bᵢ descending.  
       - Pick the top 26 as the initial set; store their indices as `selected`, the rest as `unselected`.

    3. **Swap Loop**  
       - For every `i_unsel` in `unselected` and every `i_sel` in `selected`, build a new candidate list replacing `i_sel` with `i_unsel`.  
       - Call the LP routine to obtain radii and compute the sum.  
       - Track the best swap and its sum.  
       - If the best swap improves the current sum, perform it: update `selected` and `unselected`.  
       - Repeat until a full pass over all possible swaps yields no improvement.

    4. **Fine‑tuning SA**  
       - Initialise a `rng` seed for reproducibility.  
       - For `k` iterations (≈ 5000), pick a random index `idx` in the selected set, perturb its (x,y) by a small random offset (step size decaying from 0.10 to 0.001, temperature from 1.0 to 1e‑4).  
       - Re‑solve the LP for the perturbed set, accept or reject the move with the usual Metropolis rule, and keep the best configuration seen.  

    5. **Return**  
       - The best centre coordinates, the associated LP radii, and their sum.

6.  **Numerical choices & safety**

    * **LP Solver** – `scipy.optimize.linprog` with `method='highs'`.  The solver tolerances are tight enough that the radii satisfy the constraints to within 10⁻⁸, comfortably inside the 10⁻¹² tolerance of the validator.  
    * **Clipping** – After solving, clip radii to [0, bᵢ] to guard against tiny numerical overshoots.  
    * **Determinism** – All RNG steps are seeded with a fixed integer (`seed=42`).  
    * **Performance** – With < 40 × 40 candidate swaps and single‑core execution, the total runtime stays well below one second on the provided platform.

7.  **Why this works**

    * The LP guarantees optimal radii for any chosen center set; we are therefore searching the exact space of packings defined by those centres.  
    * The greedy‑plus‑swap procedure is a classic combinatorial optimisation routine that efficiently explores the space of discrete candidate sets.  It naturally favours points that are well‑spaced (large distances) while keeping them near the centre where the boundary allows larger radii.  
    * The final local perturbation stage provides the flexibility to escape from a strictly discrete optimum and squeeze additional radius out of the geometry.

8.  **Potential failure modes**

    * **Too few candidates** – If the random candidate pool is too small or not well‑distributed, the best set may simply be suboptimal.  Using 45 random points plus the center diversifies the pool enough.  
    * **LP infeasibility** – The LP is always feasible because at worst all radii can be set to zero.  Numerical infeasibility is virtually impossible with HiGHS.  
    * **Local optima in swap loop** – Rare, but the subsequent SA stage mitigates this.  
    * **Runtime explosion** – With the chosen candidate size the number of LP solves is on the order of thousands, easily fitting within the 1‑CPU time limit.
