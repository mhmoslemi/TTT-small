**Core Idea – Force‑based constructive packing**

Unlike the previous optimisation‑centric approaches, we will *build* the packing by simulating a physical system of circles that are continuously allowed to grow while being pushed apart by their neighbours and a wall‑repulsion force.  
When the system reaches equilibrium all circles are either tangent to at least one other circle or to the boundary; at this point the sum of radii cannot be increased any further without violating a constraint.

The algorithm is a deterministic iterative procedure that:

* starts from a highly redundant tiny configuration,
* performs alternated *growth* (radii expansion) and *relaxation* (adjustment of centres),
* uses only elementary geometric queries (*distances*),
* and refines the solution by optional random jitter and a global scaling step.

Because no high‑level optimisation routine is called, the method is conceptually and implementation‑wise distinct from the previous SLSQP / linear‑programming solutions.

---

### 1. Geometry and initial configuration

1. **Unit square**: The domain is \([0,1]\times[0,1]\).

2. **Circle data structures**  
   For each circle `i (0 ≤ i < 26)` we store:
   * centre `c[i] = (x[i], y[i])` – a 2‑D vector,
   * radius `r[i]` – a positive scalar.

3. **Random initial positions**  
   * Draw `c[i]` uniformly from \([0.05, 0.95]^2\).  
   * Initialise all radii to a very small value `r0 = 1e-3` (much smaller than the expected optimum).  
   The 0.05 offset guarantees that walls are far enough so that the first growth step is unconstrained.

4. **Seeded reproducibility**  
   All random numbers are produced from a `numpy.random.default_rng` seeded deterministically in each of several restarts (e.g., 10 restarts with seeds 0…9).  This yields reproducible data while allowing exploration of different local minima.

---

### 2. Core iterative loop

For each restart, we iterate until convergence or a fixed cap of iterations (e.g., 10 000).  One iteration consists of three stages:

#### 2.1 Growth stage – simultaneous radius expansion

* For each circle `i` we compute the nearest blocking distance:

  * **Boundary distance**:
    \[
    d_{\text{edge},i} = \min\bigl\{x[i],\, 1-x[i],\, y[i],\, 1-y[i]\bigr\}.
    \]
  
  * **Neighbour distance**:  
    For every `j != i`, compute  
    \[
    d_{ij} = \|c[i]-c[j]\|_2 \;-\; r[j].
    \]
    This is the clearance left around `i` if `j`’s radius were already fixed.  
    We store the **minimum** of all these values:
    \[
    d_{\text{min},i}= \min_j d_{ij} .
    \]

  * The **maximum permissible radius** for `i` is  
    \[
    r_{\max,i} = \min\bigl\{ d_{\text{edge},i},\, d_{\text{min},i}\bigr\}.
    \]

* **Growth increment**  
  Define a growth factor `γ = 1.5` and a small tolerance `τ = 1e‑6`.  
  Update the radius as

  \[
  r[i] \; \leftarrow \; \min\bigl\{\, r[i] + γ \,(r_{\max,i} - r[i]),\; r_{\max,i}\,\bigr\}.
  \]

  This pushes the radius toward its locally available maximum but only by a fraction `γ` of the remaining slack.  The fraction keeps the system stable even when several circles compete for the same space.

#### 2.2 Relaxation stage – resolve tiny overlaps

After the growth step, some circles may still overlap due to the coupling between the growth of many objects.  We correct this by a *pairwise repulsion*:

* For each unordered pair `i<j` compute  
  \[
  d_{ij} = \|c[i]-c[j]\|_2, \qquad 
  \delta_{ij} = r[i] + r[j] - d_{ij} \; (\ge 0).
  \]
  If `δ_{ij}` is negative or smaller than `τ`, the pair is non‑overlapping and is skipped.

* Else, we shift both centres along the line connecting them:

  \[
  \Delta = \frac{\delta_{ij}}{2}\;\frac{c[i]-c[j]}{d_{ij}}.
  \]
  Update  

  \[
  c[i] \; \leftarrow \; c[i] + \mu\,\Delta, \qquad 
  c[j] \; \leftarrow \; c[j] - \mu\,\Delta,
  \]
  with a modest damping factor `μ = 0.3` to prevent oscillations.

* After all pairs are processed, perform a **boundary push**:
  * For each circle `i` that violates the boundary constraint (i.e., `r[i] > d_{\text{edge},i}`), shift it back toward the centre of the square by  
    \[
    c[i] \; \leftarrow \; c[i] + \nu\, (0.5- c[i]),\qquad
    \nu = 0.5,
    \]
    and clamp its radius to the new edge distance.

#### 2.3 Early‑termination test

Compute the maximum radius change over the last iteration  
`maxΔ = max_i |r[i] - r_old[i]|`.  
If `maxΔ < ε = 5e‑6` *and* the maximum overlap `max(δ_{ij})` is below `τ`, the system is deemed converged and the loop terminates.

---

### 3. Random jitter and restarts

Even with the above stabilisation, the system can become trapped in a local optimum that is sub‑optimal with respect to the global sum of radii.  To mitigate this:

1. **Random jitter**:  
   When the system has been stable for `K = 500` consecutive iterations, we randomly select `P = 5` circles and add a small Gaussian displacement (`σ = 0.02`) to their centres.  Afterwards the entire iterative loop (growth + relaxation) restarts from that perturbed state.  The perturbation is damped by a probability threshold to avoid excessive wandering.

2. **Multiple restarts**:  
   The whole process (random initialisation → iterative loop) is executed `R = 10` times, each with a different RNG seed.  After all restarts, the configuration with the largest sum of radii is chosen as the final answer.

---

### 4. Global scaling (optional refinement)

After convergence in a given restart, it is possible that the outermost circles are far from touching the boundary.  We can apply a *uniform homothety* that maps the outer extremities to the square edges:

* Compute the minimum and maximum of all coordinates:  
  `xmin = min_i x[i]`, `xmax = max_i x[i]`, similarly for `y`.

* Determine scaling factors:  
  \[
  s_x = \frac{1}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}},\qquad
  s_y = \frac{1}{y_{\max} - y_{\min}}.
  \]

* Use the smaller factor `s = min(s_x, s_y)` to expand the whole configuration:

  \[
  c[i] \;\leftarrow\; (c[i] - (x_{\min}, y_{\min})) \; s + (x_{\min}, y_{\min}),\qquad
  r[i] \;\leftarrow\; r[i] \, s.
  \]

* After scaling, a final round of growth–relaxation is run for a small number of iterations (10) to allow the radii to adjust to the tighter boundaries.  This step usually raises the total radius by a few‑percent without touching any constraints.

### 5. Numerical tolerances and safety checks

* **Edge tolerances**  
  All comparisons to zero or to each other use a guard `1e‑9` to protect against floating‑point noise.

* **Clipping**  
  After every centre update we clip each coordinate to `[0, 1]`.  Radii are clipped to `[0, 0.5]`.

* **Feasibility**  
  At the end of each restart, before accepting the result, we run the supplied `validate_packing` routine.  If it fails, the retry is discarded and the algorithm proceeds to the next restart.

### 6. Computational complexity

* **Per iteration**:  
  * Computing all pairwise distances costs \(O(N^2) = O(676)\).  
  * Growth and relaxation are linear or quadratic scans – negligible compared to the distance matrix.  
  * With at most 10 000 iterations in a restart, the total number of distance evaluations is about seven million – acceptable on a single CPU core (< 0.5 s on modern hardware).

* **Memory usage**:  
  Only a few arrays of size 26 or 676 are kept – < 10 kB.
