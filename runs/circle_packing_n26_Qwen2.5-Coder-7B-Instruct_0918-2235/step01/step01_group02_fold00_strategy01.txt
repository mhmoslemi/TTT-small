- Variables → 52 numbers: for each of the 26 circles we store its centre \((x_i,y_i)\) and its radius \(r_i\).
- Objective → maximise \(\sum_{i} r_i\) (equivalently, minimise \(-\sum r_i\)).

  All constraints are smooth (the square‑root is \(C^\infty\) away from the origin).  
  The problem is highly non‑convex, but modern constrained solvers such as SciPy’s **trust‑constr** or **SLSQP** can navigate it if given a good initial guess and sufficient restarts.

The key idea is that by *optimising both the radii and the centres simultaneously* we avoid the inner LP loop of the previous approach. ### 3.  Post‑processing the Solved Centres

The optimisation will produce a feasible (within tolerances) candidate \((c^*,r^*)\).  
However, because the non‑overlap constraint involves a \(\sqrt{\cdot}\), the tolerance for feasibility might leave very tiny violations (< 1e-8).  To guarantee *exact* feasibility for the validator, we perform a **one‑shot LP**:

1. Use the final centre positions \(c^*\) as fixed.
2. Solve the linear programme described in the previous strategy (constraint matrix formed from pairwise distances and boundary limits).
3. Retrieve the optimal radii \(r_{\text{opt}}\).  
   This LP is tiny (52 variables, ≈ 430 constraints) and returns exact optimal radii for the given centres.
4. Replace \(r^*\) by \(r_{\text{opt}}\).

Because the LP maximises exactly the same linear objective, the new radii can only increase (or stay the same) relative to what the solver guided us to; there is no adverse effect on the centre positions.  
Thus the final packing is guaranteed to satisfy all checking conditions.
