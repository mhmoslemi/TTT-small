**Goal** – Place 26 circles inside \([0,1]^{2}\) and choose their radii so that the circles neither overlap nor leave the square, and the total sum of the radii is as large as possible.  
The strategy below is *not* a simple heuristic walk or a block‑coordinate descent with an LP sub‑problem.  
Instead it casts the whole problem as a **continuous, unconstrained optimisation** of a carefully constructed penalty‑function and solves it with a standard bound‑constrained optimiser (`L‑BFGS‑B`).  
Because the objective and all constraints are encoded analytically and evaluated in a single pass, the method is both mathematically distinct and computationally cheap.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  Decision vector and bounds

Let  
\[
X = \bigl[c_{1x},c_{1y},\dots ,c_{26x},c_{26y},\, r_{1},\dots ,r_{26}\bigr]^{\!T}
\quad\in\mathbb{R}^{78}
\]

*Bounds* –  
* \(0 \leq c_{ix},c_{iy} \leq 1\) (all centre coordinates)  
* \(0 \leq r_{i}\)            (radii, no upper bound; the soft penalty will keep them feasible)

These bounds are supplied to `scipy.optimize.minimize` with method *`L-BFGS-B`*.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Penalised objective

The true objective is \(S=\sum_{i=1}^{26} r_{i}\).  
We define the **loss** to be minimised as

\[
\mathcal{L}(X)= -S \;+\; \lambda_{\!P}\,\Phi_{\!P}(X) \;+\;
                 \lambda_{\!B}\,\Phi_{\!B}(X)
\]

where

* **Pair penalty**  
  For every unordered pair \((i,j),\,i<j\) let  
  \[
  d_{ij}= \bigl\|(c_{i}-c_{j})\bigr\|_{2}, \qquad
  o_{ij}= \max\bigl(0,\; r_{i}+r_{j}-d_{ij}\bigr)
  \]
  The pair penalty is  
  \[
  \Phi_{\!P}(X)=\sum_{i<j}\, o_{ij}^{\,2}
  \]
  (sum of squared overlap amounts).

* **Boundary penalty**  
  For each circle \(i\)
  \[
  b_{i}^{\,L}=\max\bigl(0,\, r_{i}-c_{ix}\bigr),\quad
  b_{i}^{\,R}=\max\bigl(0,\, r_{i}-(1-c_{ix})\bigr),\quad
  b_{i}^{\,B}=\max\bigl(0,\, r_{i}-c_{iy}\bigr),\quad
  b_{i}^{\,T}=\max\bigl(0,\, r_{i}-(1-c_{iy})\bigr)
  \]
  and the boundary penalty is  
  \[
  \Phi_{\!B}(X)=\sum_{i}\Bigl(
       b_{i}^{\,L\,2}+b_{i}^{\,R\,2}+b_{i}^{\,B\,2}+b_{i}^{\,T\,2}\Bigr)
  \]

* **Weights**  
  Initially set \(\lambda_{\!P} = 10^{2}\) and \(\lambda_{\!B}=10^{2}\).  
  During the optimisation these two weights are *gradually increased*:
  \[
  \lambda_{\!P}(k)=\lambda_{\!P}(0)\,(1.5)^{\,\lfloor k/100 \rfloor},
  \quad
  \lambda_{\!B}(k)=\lambda_{\!B}(0)\,(1.5)^{\,\lfloor k/100 \rfloor}
  \]
  where \(k\) is the current iteration counter.  
  The exponential growth forces the optimiser to first explore the search space (much larger radius‑gain possible) and only later becomes highly penalising for any overlap or boundary violation, driving the solution to feasibility.

### 3.  Gradient of the loss

The gradient is computed analytically to avoid costly numerical approximations.  
Key formulas (all scalars implicitly summed over indices):

* **Derivative of the pair penalty**  
  If \(o_{ij}>0\) (i.e. the circles overlap), then with  
  \[
  u_{ij}=\frac{c_{i}-c_{j}}{d_{ij}}
  \]
  we have
  \[
  \frac{\partial o_{ij}}{\partial c_{i}}   =   u_{ij}, \qquad
  \frac{\partial o_{ij}}{\partial c_{j}}   =  -u_{ij},
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial o_{ij}}{\partial r_{i}}   =   1,\qquad
  \frac{\partial o_{ij}}{\partial r_{j}}   =   1
  \]
  otherwise all derivatives are zero.  
  Thus
  \[
  \frac{\partial \Phi_{\!P}}{\partial c_{i}}
  = 2\sum_{j\neq i}\;o_{ij}\,u_{ij}, \qquad
  \frac{\partial \Phi_{\!P}}{\partial r_{i}}
  = 2\sum_{j\neq i}\;o_{ij}
  \]

* **Derivative of the boundary penalty**  
  For the left boundary term, e.g.  
  \[
  f_{i}^{L}= \max(0,\, r_{i}-c_{ix}) 
  \quad
  \frac{\partial f_{i}^{L}}{\partial c_{ix}} = -\mathbf{1}_{ f_{i}^{L}>0},\;
   \frac{\partial f_{i}^{L}}{\partial r_{i}} = \mathbf{1}_{ f_{i}^{L}>0}
  \]
  and analogously for the other three sides.  
  Summing over the four sides yields \(\frac{\partial \Phi_{\!B}}{\partial c_{i}}\) and \(\frac{\partial \Phi_{\!B}}{\partial r_{i}}\).

* **Total gradient**  
  \[
  \nabla\mathcal{L}(X) =
  \bigl[
     -\mathbf{1}_{26}\quad
     -\mathbf{1}_{26}
  \bigr]
  +\lambda_{\!P}\,\bigl[\nabla\Phi_{\!P}^{c},\;\nabla\Phi_{\!P}^{r}\bigr]
  +\lambda_{\!B}\,\bigl[\nabla\Phi_{\!B}^{c},\;\nabla\Phi_{\!B}^{r}\bigr]
  \]
  where \(\mathbf{1}_{26}\) is a vector of ones of length 26 (the derivative of \(-S\) w.r.t. each \(r_i\) is \(-1\)).

All the above are implemented with simple NumPy vectorised operations; the cost per gradient evaluation is \(O(n^2)\) with \(n=26\) (≈ 700 elementary operations), negligible compared to the line search inside `L‑BFGS‑B`.
