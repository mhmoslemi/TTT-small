\subsection*{$SPO^2$ versus RS-GRPO}

\paragraph{The problem with RS-GRPO.}
For a group of $n$ sampled programs, RS-GRPO transforms each reward as
\[
Z_i=\exp(\beta_g R_i)
\]
and compares it with a leave-one-out baseline from the same group,
\[
B_{-i}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}Z_j,
\qquad
A_i^{\mathrm{GRPO}}
=\frac{1}{\beta_g}\left(\frac{Z_i}{B_{-i}}-1\right).
\]
This has four failure modes.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The signal is relative only to the current group. The same reward can
    receive a positive advantage in a weak group and a negative advantage in a
    strong group.
    \item A tied group gives zero advantage to every program, even when the
    whole group is better than programs found earlier.
    \item A small group gives a noisy estimate of the exponential reward
    moment. One lucky program can control the normalization of all its peers.
    \item The implementation changes $\beta_g$ separately for each group to
    satisfy a KL target. Therefore different groups optimize different levels
    of risk sensitivity. In the observed run, the median $\beta_g$ grew from
    about $1.19$ at step 0 to the cap of $1{,}048{,}576$ by step 20. At that
    scale, the update is nearly winner-take-all.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{$SPO^2$ Solution.}
$SPO^2$ does not construct a new baseline from the other
programs in each group. It first transforms rewards:
\[
Z_i=e^{\beta R_i},
\]
then compares $Z_i$ with a persistent, run-wide value
$V_{t-1}$:
\[
A_i^{\mathrm{SPO\mbox{-}RS}}
=
\frac{1}{\beta}
\left(
\frac{e^{\beta R_i}}{V_{t-1}}-1
\right).
\]
Every program generated in step $t$ uses the same $V_{t-1}$. Therefore the
current programs cannot change one another's advantages.

Only after all advantages for the current step have been computed, the value
estimate is updated:
\[
\overline{Z}_t=\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{i=1}^{n_t}Z_i,
\qquad
V_t=(1-\eta_t)V_{t-1}+\eta_t\overline{Z}_t.
\]
Its memory changes according to the KL divergence between consecutive policies.
When the policy changes substantially, old evidence is forgotten faster. When
the policy is stable, the tracker keeps more history.

This fixes the GRPO failures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item One rollout is enough to obtain an advantage.
    \item A tied batch can still learn: all candidates are reinforced if they
    beat historical performance and suppressed if they fall below it.
    \item The meaning of positive advantage stays consistent across parents
    and steps.
    \item Reward normalization uses thousands of historical samples instead
    of one small group.
    \item Risk sensitivity remains tied to one fixed scientific objective.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Claim.}
$SPO^2$ replaces a noisy, peer-dependent, and changing entropic GRPO signal with
a history-anchored estimator whose expected update follows one fixed entropic
objective. In our runs, this change is associated with substantially better
sample efficiency and a slightly better best construction.


\subsection*{Bandit allocation versus fixed allocation}

\paragraph{The failure without a bandit.}
A fixed allocator gives each strategy the same number of rollouts, even after
the pilot samples show that the strategies have different probabilities and
different sizes of improvement. This wastes rollouts on strategies with weak
upper tails.

\paragraph{The bandit solution.}
Suppose the strategist proposes strategies $s\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$. Here, $s$
denotes one concrete search plan. For each strategy, define improvement over
the parent as
\[
X_s=\max(R_{\mathrm{new}}-R_{\mathrm{parent}},0),
\qquad
p_s=\Pr(X_s>0\mid s).
\]
The hurdle bandit models two quantities separately:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the probability $p_s$ that strategy $s$ improves the parent;
    \item the size of the improvement, conditional on success.
\end{itemize}
Invalid programs and valid non-improvements both have $X_s=0$.

After giving every strategy a small pilot budget, the bandit assigns each
remaining rollout to the strategy with the largest estimated increase in the
expected final best reward. If $b$ is the best improvement already observed
and $n_s$ is the number of additional rollouts assigned to strategy $s$, the
target is
\[
\mathbb{E}
\left[
\max\left(
b,
\max_s\max_{1\leq j\leq n_s}X_{s,j}
\right)
\right].
\]
This is the correct objective for scientific discovery because success is
measured by the best construction found, rather than the average reward of all
rollouts.

\paragraph{Empirical evidence.}
Across 72 parent-level allocation decisions, the bandit assigned all follow-up
rollouts to one strategy in 67 cases. The follow-up rollouts improved on the
best pilot result in 32 of 72 cases. Among these, 19 improvements were larger
than $10^{-7}$ and 13 were larger than $10^{-6}$. Follow-up rollouts accounted
for $37.5\%$ of all attempts but produced the best construction in 4 of 9
completed training steps.

At step 3 for one parent, the selected strategy had the lowest pilot validity
among the four strategies but the best pilot reward. The bandit assigned it 24
additional rollouts, improving $C_5$ from $0.380907442$ to $0.380903253$. This
shows that the allocator targets promising upper tails rather than simply
selecting the strategy with the highest validity rate.

\paragraph{Claim.}
The hurdle bandit directs follow-up compute toward the strategy with the
largest estimated contribution to the final best result. The run logs show
that follow-up allocation repeatedly improved on the pilot result. A matched
uniform-allocation run is still needed to establish a causal improvement over
uniform sampling.


\subsection*{Strategist versus no strategist}

\paragraph{The failure without a strategist.}
Without a strategist, the coder samples a program directly:
\[
y\sim\pi_\theta(y\mid x).
\]
The same policy must both invent a high-level idea and implement it. Once
training finds an easy high-reward pattern, the coder can increase reward by
repeatedly copying that pattern. This produces collapse in the space of
solutions even when token entropy remains high.

\paragraph{RL cheating by the coder.}
The parent is already a high-scoring construction. Copying the parent, or
making a negligible change to it, is therefore a low-risk way for the coder to
obtain a high reward. When other programs in the group are worse or crash, the
copied program can receive a positive advantage, so RL reinforces the copying
behavior. The coder learns to preserve the current answer instead of
discovering a new one. We call this \emph{RL cheating}: the policy improves its
training signal without producing a genuine scientific improvement.

\paragraph{The strategist solution.}
With a strategist, the search becomes hierarchical:
\[
z\sim q_\phi(z\mid x,H),
\qquad
y\sim\pi_\theta(y\mid x,z),
\]
where $z$ is a proposed plan and $H$ is the search history. The strategist
continues to propose high-level directions while the trained coder learns to
implement them. This separates idea generation from code generation and keeps
multiple semantic search directions available as the coder specializes. It
also makes the parent-copy shortcut harder to exploit because the coder is
conditioned on an explicit proposal for what to investigate or change.
