Background: LLM-Based Scientific Discovery

A large language model (LLM) writes a program that constructs a candidate solution. A task-specific evaluator checks the construction and assigns its score.

Search with a fixed model

The search reuses promising solutions as context for new programs. The model’s weights stay unchanged.

Search with reinforcement learning

Evaluation also supplies a learning signal. The LLM updates its weights to favor programs with stronger outcomes.

Parent: a scored starting solution. Rollout: one generated program. Archive: retained solutions and their ancestry.

Success means finding a better valid construction, even if many attempts fail.
A rollout is one generated candidate program and its evaluation. The environment, not the LLM itself, executes the program. The raw problem score can be minimized or maximized. Training rewards use a larger-is-better convention.

Prior Art: Candidate Sampling and Search

  1. Uniform sampling: generate the same number of programs from each selected parent, regardless of early results.
  2. Evolutionary and island-based search: populations retain and mutate strong evaluated candidates. Separate populations help maintain diversity.
  3. Tree-based search: select a starting solution by balancing observed quality with exploration. UCT illustrates this tradeoff:
U(s)=Q(s)+clogN1+n(s)

s: archived state    Q(s): observed quality    n(s): expansions of s
N: total expansions    c: exploration strength    U(s): parent-selection score

Parent selection chooses a starting solution. The LLM then generates candidate programs from that context.

Technical Problems in Existing Approaches

01

One generation combines algorithm selection and implementation

The LLM chooses an approach while writing the program. Additional samples can repeat the same approach without explicitly testing alternatives.

02

Reinforcement learning can favor copies of an existing solution

Reproducing an existing construction can still earn a high reward. Reinforcing these outputs can make the LLM generate more copies and fewer distinct constructions.

03

A fixed sampling split cannot adapt to early evaluation results

Equal sampling continues after early results indicate that some directions are more promising. The remaining budget cannot shift toward those directions.

04

Group-relative training can discard useful reward information

When all programs in a group receive the same reward, their group-relative advantages are zero. This removes the reward-based update even if that reward improves on earlier results.

The first point concerns direct program generation without explicit algorithm-level sampling choices. It does not claim that a single LLM cannot produce different algorithms. The second is a conditional reinforcement mechanism: copying a strong construction can remain rewarding, and reinforcing such outputs can reduce construction-level exploration. It does not establish that RL always causes collapse. The third concerns fixed, equal allocation rather than adaptive search methods in general; early evidence can be uncertain. The fourth concerns group-relative advantages formed by centering rewards on the current group's mean. Exactly equal rewards produce zero reward advantages, although a separate KL regularizer may still contribute gradients. A historical reference can assign a shared nonzero advantage to that group but cannot rank identical rewards within it. These are specific limitations motivating the proposed method, not universal claims about every prior system.

Technical Solution: Hierarchical Discovery Pipeline

Strategist: frozen planning LLM. Coder: shared LLM trained on all evaluated programs.

Source: supplied presentation, Page 7. The proposed hierarchy separates two roles: the strategist is a frozen planning LLM, and the coder is a trainable program-generating LLM. Archive states include programs, constructions, scores, and lineage. The parent selector picks P states. A frozen strategist generates S sequential, distinct plans for each parent. Each coder prompt receives only its own parent and one extracted plan, not the other strategies or hidden strategist reasoning. Equal pilots precede bandit allocation of each parent's fixed remaining budget. Top-r per strategy then top-K per parent feed the archive. Pilot and phase-2 programs train the coder. The coder may correct scientific or interface errors in a strategy. Animation illustrates dependencies, not measured timings.

Technical Solution: Allocation and Policy Training

Parent-relative bandit allocation

  • Generate H pilot programs for each of S strategies.
  • Estimate each plan’s chance of improving on its parent and the size of successful gains.
  • Allocate B follow-ups by their expected effect on the best sampled reward, accounting for uncertainty.
Zk,j=max⁡(Rk,j−Rp,0)

Rk,j: reward of pilot j for strategy k. Rp: parent reward.
Zk,j: improvement over the parent, set to zero for invalid outputs.

ak∈ℤ≥0,∑kak=B Nparent=SH+B

ak: follow-ups for strategy k.
Nparent: total planned programs for that parent.

Historical-baseline training (SPO²)

  • Higher rewards receive exponentially larger weights.
  • Compute every advantage against the same moving average of earlier transformed rewards.
G(r)=eβr,β>0
At,i=1β(eβrt,ivt−1−1)

vt−1: moving average of earlier G(r). β: strength of reward shaping.
rt,i: reward. At,i: training weight for rollout i at step t.

Positive A reinforces a program. Negative A discourages it. A tied group can still receive a nonzero weight relative to earlier rewards. Hold β and the baseline fixed within the step, then update the baseline.

R and r denote larger-is-better training rewards. Scientific plots show the raw task score, minimized for the Erdős problem.

Source: supplied presentation, Page 8. H is pilot count per strategy, S strategy count, B the parent's fixed follow-up budget, and a_k integer follow-up allocations. The hurdle model uses strict parent-relative improvement and conditional positive gain. Invalid candidates are zero gain. Expected-best allocation targets the upper tail, not the mean. Fixed-beta SPO² uses transformed rewards and a shared historical moving reference with policy-adaptive retention. A common baseline can assign nonzero advantages to a tied group relative to history but cannot rank identical rewards within that group. Policy parameters remain fixed until accumulated gradients and required evaluation results are ready.

Technical Solution: Dependency-Aware Execution

Evaluate programs as they arrive. Once generation ends, train on evaluated programs while CPU evaluation continues. Keep parameters fixed until the step’s final optimizer update.

Sources: supplied presentation, Page 9, and pipeline_implementation.tex. Completed programs enter CPU evaluation as generation continues. Phase 2 may start when an individual parent's pilot evaluations are ready. In the shared-GPU layout, training begins after generation and required policy scoring, while remaining CPU evaluations continue. Reward-ready backward work can proceed without waiting for unrelated pending evaluations. Parameters are not updated during rollout generation or partial gradient accumulation. One optimizer update follows all required gradients. The historical normalization is frozen for the step. The bar lengths are schematic and do not establish measured speedup or continuous 100% GPU utilization.

Distinguishing Features of the Idea

  • Separate planning from learning: the strategist proposes plans with fixed weights. Only the shared coder learns from evaluated programs.
  • Compare each plan with its parent: estimate both improvement probability and gain size, so rare large gains enter the allocation decision.
  • Allocate after observing the pilots: use their results to choose follow-up counts while keeping the total program budget fixed.
  • Use one historical baseline across groups: compare outputs with earlier rewards. Larger policy changes reduce the weight of older rewards.
  • Account for related archive states: update ancestor expansion counts and avoid selecting a solution with its own descendant in the same batch.
  • Overlap work without changing the policy: train on evaluated outputs while other evaluations continue, then apply one final update.
Source: supplied presentation, Page 10. Lineage is the recorded parent–child ancestry. PUCT combines best-child reward, archive priors, and expansion counts for parent selection. The hierarchy, allocator, training reference, archive controls, and execution dependencies form the technical disclosure. Internal records can show which mechanisms a system implements. Legal novelty and claim scope require a separate closest-reference assessment.
